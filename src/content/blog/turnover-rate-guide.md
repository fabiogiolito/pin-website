Employee turnover rate is the percentage of workers who leave your organization over a given period, calculated as (separations / average headcount) x 100. U.S. voluntary turnover averaged 13.0% in [Mercer's 2025 US Turnover Survey](https://www.imercer.com/articleinsights/workforce-turnover-trends) of 2,617 organizations, while BLS data puts total separations across the economy near 40% a year. [Gallup estimates](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/247391/fixable-problem-costs-businesses-trillion.aspx) voluntary departures cost U.S. businesses roughly $1 trillion per year, with replacement running 0.5x to 2x a departing employee's annual salary.

What makes that number even more frustrating? According to the [Work Institute's 2025 Retention Report](https://workinstitute.com/retention-reports/), 76.3% of employee exits in 2024 were preventable. Three out of every four departures didn't have to happen. This guide gives you the exact formula, current benchmarks by industry and role level, the real reasons people quit, and eight strategies that actually move the needle.

## What Is Employee Turnover Rate?

Employee turnover rate measures how many workers leave your organization during a specific period relative to your average headcount. According to [SHRM](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/tools/forms/turnover-cost-calculation-spreadsheet), it's the single most-tracked workforce metric after headcount itself - and for good reason. Every departure triggers recruiting costs, onboarding time, and productivity losses that compound quickly.

But not all turnover is the same. You need to track these categories separately because they require completely different responses:

-   **Voluntary turnover** - employees resign on their own terms (new job, retirement, relocation, dissatisfaction). This is the number you can actually influence. It accounts for roughly two-thirds of all separations in most organizations.
-   **Involuntary turnover** - employer-initiated separations including terminations for cause, layoffs, and restructuring. BLS JOLTS data for 2024 shows a monthly layoff/discharge rate of 1.1%, or roughly 1.7 million workers per month.
-   **Regrettable turnover** - high performers and hard-to-replace specialists who leave voluntarily. This is the most expensive category and the one most worth preventing.
-   **Early turnover** - departures within the first 90 days, which [InsightGlobal research cited by AIHR](https://www.aihr.com/blog/employee-onboarding-statistics/) pegs at 22% of all new hires. Early exits almost always signal hiring or onboarding failures.

How does turnover differ from attrition? In practice, most HR teams use the terms interchangeably. Technically, [attrition](https://www.pin.com/blog/attrition-rate-guide/) refers to departures where the role isn't backfilled, while turnover assumes replacement hiring. Both terms share identical formulas and industry benchmarks.

We've noticed that the biggest surprise for our customers isn't how much turnover costs. It's how much of that cost compounds during the gap between someone leaving and their replacement reaching full productivity. According to Pin's 2026 user survey, recruiters using Pin fill open roles in an average of 14 days, versus the 39-day median SHRM reports for nonexecutive roles ([SHRM 2026](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/research/recruiting-benchmarking)). That speed difference matters because a vacancy that closes in two weeks avoids the workload spike that typically pushes a second team member toward the exit. We've also found that the fastest-recovering teams aren't the ones who scramble hardest when someone quits - they're the ones who kept an external pipeline warm alongside their retention programs. Once a role opens, they move immediately. Pairing strong retention practices with fast replacement capacity means turnover stops cascading.

## How Do You Calculate Employee Turnover Rate?

SHRM, Mercer, and virtually every HRIS platform use the same formula, as documented in [SHRM's benchmarking methodology](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/tools/forms/turnover-cost-calculation-spreadsheet):

**Turnover Rate (%) = (Number of Separations During Period / Average Number of Employees During Period) x 100**

Where average headcount = (headcount at period start + headcount at period end) / 2.

### Worked Example: Monthly Calculation

Your company started April with 400 employees and ended with 388. During the month, 18 people left - 12 resigned voluntarily and 6 were terminated.

-   Average headcount: (400 + 388) / 2 = 394
-   Total separation rate: (18 / 394) x 100 = **4.57%**
-   Voluntary departure rate: (12 / 394) x 100 = **3.05%**

### How to Annualize Correctly

Don't multiply a monthly rate by 12. Simple multiplication ignores compounding and seasonal hiring spikes. Instead, apply the formula across the full 12-month window using total annual separations divided by average headcount for the year. When projecting from a single month for board presentations, use this adjustment:

**Annualized Rate = 1 - (1 - monthly rate)^12**

Applying the formula to the 4.57% monthly rate above gives: 1 - (1 - 0.0457)^12 = **43.2%** annualized - significantly different from the naive 4.57% x 12 = 54.8% you'd get from simple multiplication.

### What Counts as a Separation?

Include all permanent employee departures: resignations, terminations, retirements, and deaths. Exclude contractors, temps, internal transfers (they stayed in the organization), and employees on leave. For voluntary-only calculations, exclude layoffs and terminations from the numerator but keep the same denominator.

## What Is the Average Employee Turnover Rate?

The average employee turnover rate in the U.S. is about 13% a year for voluntary departures, per [Mercer's 2025 US Turnover Survey](https://www.imercer.com/articleinsights/workforce-turnover-trends). Counting every separation across the economy, [BLS JOLTS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/jolts.t20.htm) puts it at roughly 40% a year. Both numbers are correct. They measure different things.

- **Employer surveys (Mercer, Payscale)** report the typical organization's rate. Mercer's 13.0% excludes retirees, volunteers, and contractors. [Payscale's 2026 Compensation Best Practices Report](https://www.payscale.com/content/report/2026-compensation-best-practice-report.pdf) puts the median employer at 12% total and 8% voluntary turnover for 2025.
- **BLS JOLTS** counts every separation from every U.S. payroll: 62.8 million in 2025, against a monthly average separation rate of 3.3%. Short-tenure hourly jobs in hospitality and retail can turn over several times a year, which pulls the economy-wide figure far above what a typical salaried employer sees. Quits alone ran about 24% of employment.

So what's normal? For a salaried, office-based organization, a total rate of 10-15% and a voluntary rate under 10% sit near the middle of the pack. Frontline-heavy employers should compare themselves to the BLS figure for their own sector instead (see the table below). A rate is only "high" or "low" relative to the right peer group.

Comparing your company against a national average requires same-sector benchmarking - a 35% rate is well below the norm for a restaurant group and well above it for an insurer.

Three specialized benchmarks help with narrower comparisons. Mercer's 2025 survey puts voluntary turnover at 26.7% in retail and wholesale (the highest sector) and 8.2% in insurance and reinsurance (the lowest). Hospitals have their own benchmark: the [NSI 2026 National Health Care Retention Report](https://www.nsinursingsolutions.com/Documents/Library/NSI_National_Health_Care_Retention_Report.pdf), covering 527 hospitals, puts 2025 registered nurse turnover at 17.6% and total hospital staff turnover at 18.5%. Ravio's 2026 compensation data puts European tech attrition at 17.4%, with engineering the lowest function at 12%.

### Turnover by Job Level

Seniority dramatically affects turnover rates. [Mercer's 2025 data](https://www.imercer.com/articleinsights/workforce-turnover-trends) breaks it down by level:

-   **Executives:** 5.2% voluntary turnover
-   **Management:** 6.3%
-   **Sales professionals:** 7.3%
-   **Non-sales professionals:** 9.1%
-   **Blue-collar / para-professional:** 12.5%

Senior roles naturally carry higher comp, more autonomy, and greater switching costs - which keeps executive departure rates comparatively low. When a leader does leave, though, the replacement cost rises disproportionately (more on that below).

### The Hidden Costs Most Companies Miss

Direct replacement costs (job ads, recruiter time, agency fees) are easy to track. Hidden damage comes from costs that never appear in your recruiting budget:

-   **Lost productivity** - it typically takes a new hire 6-12 months to reach the productivity level of the person they replaced. Real output loss follows.
-   **Knowledge drain** - departing employees take relationships, institutional knowledge, and undocumented processes with them.
-   **Team disruption** - remaining team members absorb extra workload during the vacancy, leading to burnout and potential secondary departures.
-   **Client impact** - in client-facing roles, turnover can damage relationships and revenue. In healthcare, [nurse turnover](/blog/healthcare-recruiting-strategies/) directly affects patient outcomes.

Speaking of healthcare: the [NSI's 2026 report](https://www.nsinursingsolutions.com/Documents/Library/NSI_National_Health_Care_Retention_Report.pdf) across 527 hospitals found the average cost of a single departing registered nurse is $60,090. Each 1% change in RN turnover saves or costs the average hospital $295,000 a year.

### How to Estimate Your Organization's Turnover Cost

Here's a quick framework you can apply to your own workforce. You don't need a complex model - a rough estimate is enough to justify retention investments to finance and leadership.

**Step 1: Calculate your annual voluntary separations.** If you have 500 employees and a 13% voluntary departure rate, that's 65 exits per year.

**Step 2: Estimate the average replacement cost per employee.** Use [Gallup's multipliers](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/646538/employee-turnover-preventable-often-ignored.aspx): 40% of salary for frontline, 80% for mid-level, 200% for leaders. If your average salary is $75,000 and most departures are mid-level, estimate $60,000 per replacement.

**Step 3: Multiply.** 65 departures x $60,000 = $3.9 million per year in total turnover cost for a 500-person company. This is a concrete number to take to your CFO.

Now run the same math at a 10% rate instead of 13%. You'd have 50 departures, costing $3 million - a savings of $900,000 per year. This becomes the financial case for every retention initiative in this article. Even a 3-percentage-point improvement pays for itself many times over.

### The Flight-Risk Warning Signs

How do you spot turnover before it happens? As of Q4 2025, [Gallup tracked](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/703280/worker-thriving-declines-job-market-pessimism-grows.aspx) 51% of U.S. employees actively looking for or watching for new job opportunities - just below the 52% record Gallup measured in Q3 2025. Among Gen Z workers, that figure climbs to 61%.

Common warning signs that correlate with flight risk include:

-   Declining participation in meetings and team activities
-   Reduced output quality or missed deadlines
-   Withdrawal from optional projects or committees
-   Increased PTO usage, especially in short bursts (interview time)
-   Updated LinkedIn profile (the classic tell)

None of these individually confirm an exit. But a cluster of three or more, combined with the 51% baseline looking rate, strongly suggests a proactive stay conversation is worth the 30-minute investment. Such a conversation costs essentially nothing. Ignoring the signs and losing a high performer can easily run into six figures.

## 8 Data-Backed Strategies to Reduce Employee Turnover

The [Work Institute's 2025 report](https://workinstitute.com/retention-reports/) draws on 123,297 exit interviews collected since 2019 and found that 76.3% of 2024 departures were preventable. Management-related turnover hit a six-year high (9.7% of exits) in their data. Here are eight strategies backed by research - not theory.

### 1\. Fix Your Managers First

In the [2025 State of the Global Workplace report](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/659279/global-engagement-falls-second-time-2009.aspx), Gallup found that 70% of team engagement is attributable to the manager. Yet manager engagement itself fell from 30% to 27% globally in 2024, and only 44% of supervisors received formal training. Gallup's [2026 edition](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/349484/state-of-the-global-workplace.aspx) shows manager engagement sliding again, to 22% in 2025. You can't reduce employee turnover without fixing the manager layer first.

What works: structured manager training on feedback, one-on-one frequency, and career development conversations. Companies that invest in manager capability see measurable engagement lifts within two quarters.

### 2\. Get Onboarding Right

With 22% of new hires leaving within 90 days and 86% making their stay-or-go decision within six months ([AIHR](https://www.aihr.com/blog/employee-onboarding-statistics/)), [onboarding](https://www.pin.com/blog/employee-onboarding-guide/) is your highest-return retention investment. Sixty percent of early departures cite inadequate or disorganized training as their primary reason for leaving, per the same AIHR data.

Four in five workers say they'd stay longer in a role with a better onboarding process. And when managers actively participate in onboarding, new hires are 3.4x more likely to rate the experience as exceptional. Structure the first 90 days with clear milestones, assign a peer buddy, and make sure the hiring manager is present - not just HR.

### 3\. Pay Competitively (and Transparently)

Compensation or benefits would have kept 30% of voluntary leavers in Gallup's data, and [BambooHR's 2024 Compensation Trends](https://www.bamboohr.com/resources/data-at-work/data-stories/2024-compensation-trends) survey found that 73% of employees would consider leaving their current job for a higher paycheck. You don't always need to be the highest payer - but you need to be close, and you need to be transparent about it.

Transparency changes how employees talk about their pay. In [Payscale's 2026 Compensation Best Practices Report](https://www.payscale.com/featured-content/cbpr) (3,413 respondents), organizations that communicate pay proactively and transparently had 9% detractors, versus 20% across all organizations.

### 4\. Offer Flexible and Hybrid Work

A randomized controlled trial by Stanford researchers, published in [Nature in 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07500-2), found that hybrid work (two days per week from home) cut voluntary attrition by 33% with zero negative impact on performance or promotion rates. Researchers followed 1,612 employees at a large multinational - experimental evidence, not self-reporting.

Where industry and roles allow it, hybrid flexibility is one of the most cost-effective retention tools available - and requires no salary increase.

## 4 More Strategies to Build Retention Systems

### 5\. Create Clear Career Paths

[Deloitte's 2025 Global Human Capital Trends](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/topics/talent/human-capital-trends/2025.html) surveyed nearly 10,000 leaders across 93 countries. Organizations that increase workers' capacity to grow and think deeply were 1.8x more likely to report better financial results. Career development isn't just a retention play - it's a business performance strategy.

Practical steps: map progression paths for every role, conduct quarterly career conversations (separate from performance reviews), and fund skill development with a per-employee learning budget. Internal mobility programs give employees a reason to stay when they get restless - the alternative is them finding growth somewhere else.

### 6\. Run Stay Interviews (Not Just Exit Interviews)

Exit interviews happen too late. By the time someone is walking out the door, you're collecting postmortem data, not saving the relationship. Stay interviews flip the script: a quarterly or semi-annual conversation with current employees about what keeps them here and what might push them to leave.

Remember: 45% of leavers told Gallup that no leader discussed their job satisfaction in their final three months. Stay interviews directly solve that gap. They're free, take 20-30 minutes, and the data is immediately actionable.

### 7\. Monitor and Act on Engagement Data

Annual engagement surveys are too slow. By the time you collect, analyze, and act on the results, the flight risks have already left. Pulse surveys (monthly or quarterly) give you a real-time read on team sentiment.

According to the [SHRM 2025 State of the Workplace](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/research/2025-shrm-state-of-the-workplace) report (surveying 1,615 HR professionals and 471 workers), employee experience and engagement account for 42% of turnover intent, citing SHRM's earlier employee-experience research. Workers with highly effective managers were nearly 2x as likely to report job satisfaction. Track these metrics the way you'd track [recruiter KPIs](https://www.pin.com/blog/recruiter-kpis-dashboard/) - with dashboards and clear accountability.

### 8\. Reduce Time-to-Fill When Turnover Happens

Even with the best retention strategies, some turnover is unavoidable. When it happens, the speed of your backfill directly impacts the cascading costs. Every open day means extra workload on the remaining team, which drives more burnout, which drives more exits. Vicious cycles follow.

AI recruiting tools can compress the sourcing-to-hire timeline from weeks to days. Pin's AI scans 850M+ candidate profiles - the largest multi-source candidate database in the industry - to surface qualified replacements fast. With automated outreach delivering 5x better response rates and built-in interview scheduling, Pin fills vacancies in an average of 14 days rather than the typical 30-45. For teams that need to contain the cascading damage of turnover, [Pin is the go-to AI recruiting platform](https://www.pin.com/). Closing each vacancy faster means less workload pressure builds on the remaining team.

As executive recruiter Rich Rosen of Cornerstone Search puts it: _"Absolutely money maker for Recruiters... in 6 months I can directly attribute over $250K in revenue to Pin."_ When turnover creates urgent openings, having a sourcing tool that delivers qualified candidates quickly isn't just convenient - it directly limits the financial bleed.

Monthly quit rates tell the same story. The rate averaged 2.4% in 2023, 2.1% in 2024, and 2.0% in 2025, and the latest [BLS JOLTS release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/jolts.nr0.htm) shows 1.9% for July 2026, with total separations at 3.2%.

But don't confuse lower quit rates with employee satisfaction. Those same Gallup Q4 2025 figures - 51% of workers looking or watching for new roles - suggest many staff are staying put not because they're engaged, but because the job market feels uncertain. That's pent-up turnover waiting to be released. When hiring picks back up, organizations with weak retention cultures could see another wave.

And 40.3% of U.S. organizations still report difficulty hiring or retaining for certain roles, per Mercer - particularly in healthcare services, logistics, and transportation. Nor has the talent squeeze disappeared. It's just shifted to specific sectors and skill sets.

What does that mean for workforce planning? Seeing low quit rates right now? Don't celebrate prematurely. Test the waters with engagement pulse surveys. If your engagement scores are declining while departures stay flat, that's a warning sign - your people are staying out of fear, not loyalty. [Deloitte's 2025 Human Capital Trends](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/topics/talent/human-capital-trends/2025.html) report found that 41% of employee time daily is spent on work that creates no organizational value. Not just a productivity problem - it's a disengagement signal that often precedes a wave of resignations once external conditions improve.

Smart HR teams are using this low-turnover window to strengthen retention infrastructure - manager training, career pathing, compensation reviews - so they're ready when the market inevitably swings back toward candidates.

## Voluntary vs. Involuntary Turnover: Why the Distinction Matters

Tracking total departures as a single number hides the story. A 15% separation rate means something very different depending on the split. If 12% is voluntary and 3% is involuntary, you have a retention problem. If 5% is voluntary and 10% is involuntary (layoffs), you have a workforce planning or business model problem. Each scenario calls for a completely different intervention.

For national context, [BLS JOLTS annual data](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/jolts.t19.htm) counted 62.8 million U.S. job separations in 2025. Quits made up 60.6% of them, layoffs and discharges 33.8%, and other separations (retirements, deaths, disability, and transfers) the remaining 5.6%.

Best practice is to track four separate rates on your [workforce quality dashboard](https://www.pin.com/blog/quality-of-hire-metrics/):

1.  **Total separations** - all departures, your headline number for board reporting
2.  **Voluntary departures** - resignations only, your primary retention health metric
3.  **Regrettable exits** - voluntary departures of high performers and critical-role employees, the number that actually hurts
4.  **Early departures** - exits within the first 90 days, your hiring and onboarding quality signal

Segment each by department, manager, job level, and [tenure band](/blog/employee-tenure-funding-stage-study/). Aggregate numbers serve benchmarking; segmented views reveal the actionable problems.

### Why Manager-Level Segmentation Matters Most

Slice voluntary departures by manager and patterns emerge fast. Across most organizations, a small number of supervisors account for a disproportionate share of resignations. That's not a coincidence. Gallup's finding that 70% of engagement variance is manager-driven means your worst supervisors are essentially pushing people out the door.

With three managers out of twenty responsible for 60% of voluntary exits, you don't have a company-wide retention problem. You have a three-person management problem. That's a much more solvable challenge - and it's invisible if you only track turnover at the aggregate level.

Build a quarterly dashboard that shows voluntary exits per manager (indexed to team size), internal transfer requests by team, and engagement survey scores by reporting line. Wherever low engagement, high transfer requests, and high departures overlap, you've found the team that needs intervention first.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a good employee turnover rate?

It depends on your industry. The U.S. benchmark is 13% for voluntary departures (Mercer 2025), and Payscale puts the median employer at 12% total turnover. Anything below your industry benchmark is generally considered good. A professional services firm under 10% voluntary is doing well, while a restaurant group running under 50% total turnover beats the BLS sector norm of roughly 66%. Focus on reducing regrettable exits - departures of high performers - rather than hitting an arbitrary number.

### How do you calculate employee turnover rate?

Use the formula: (Number of Separations / Average Headcount) x 100. Average headcount = (starting employees + ending employees) / 2. Apply this over a month, quarter, or year. For voluntary separations specifically, only count resignations in the numerator. SHRM, Mercer, and most HRIS platforms use this same formula for benchmarking.

### How do you calculate regrettable turnover rate?

Regrettable turnover rate applies the same formula - (separations / average headcount) x 100 - but limits the numerator to voluntary departures from high performers and critical-role employees only. Your HRIS or performance review data determines who qualifies. Common thresholds: employees rated "exceeds expectations" in their last review, or workers in roles that take more than 90 days to backfill. Track regrettable turnover separately from overall voluntary exits because the interventions differ. Reducing regrettable losses requires targeted retention for your highest contributors - manager quality, compensation competitiveness, and career growth - not broad-based perks that apply equally to all staff.

### What is the difference between turnover and attrition?

The technical difference is that turnover implies the position gets backfilled with a new hire, while [workforce attrition](https://www.pin.com/blog/attrition-rate-guide/) means the role is left open or eliminated. In practice, most HR teams and researchers use the terms interchangeably. Formulas and benchmarks are identical. What matters more is tracking voluntary vs. involuntary separately.

### Is 25% turnover high?

It depends on what the 25% counts. As a total rate covering every exit, 25% is below the 2025 U.S. average of roughly 40% and in line with finance and insurance (about 25%), per BLS JOLTS. As a voluntary-only rate, 25% is nearly double Mercer's 13% benchmark and high for most salaried organizations, though still well below accommodation and food services, where quits alone run near 50% a year.

### What does a 20% turnover rate mean?

A 20% annual turnover rate means the number of departures during the year equals one-fifth of your average headcount. For a 250-person company, that's 50 exits, or roughly one every week. It doesn't always mean 20% of individual people left, because a single hard-to-fill role can turn over two or three times in a year.

### What causes high employee turnover?

Gallup's research shows better compensation or benefits would have kept 30% of voluntary leavers, more positive manager interactions 21%, and fixing frustrating organizational issues 13%. Management-related departures hit a six-year high in the Work Institute's 2025 Retention Report. Importantly, 76.3% of 2024 exits were preventable - most employees would have stayed if someone had intervened.
