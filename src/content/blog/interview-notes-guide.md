Interview notes are the written record of what a candidate said, demonstrated, and scored during an interview - and taking them well is one of the simplest ways to improve hiring accuracy.

A [Frontiers in Psychology study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7817537/) tracked 7,650 hires at a large IT company. Notes written after each interview and matched against the job's competencies were linked to higher performance ratings (β=0.012, p<0.05) and lower turnover (β=−0.233, p<0.01). Without a written record, you're relying on recall: classic research found unrehearsed details fade from short-term memory within about 18 seconds ([Peterson & Peterson](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14432252/), 1959).

Why does this matter so much? Two-thirds (66%) of managers and executives say most recent hires aren't fully prepared for their roles, according to [Deloitte's 2025 Global Human Capital Trends](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/about/press-room/deloitte-report-aims-to-help-leaders-navigate-complex-workplace-tensions.html) survey of nearly 10,000 leaders. First-year turnover is the metric that exposes a weak hiring call, and it moves quickly. Across 6,640 employers in [Employ's 2026 Recruiting Benchmarks Report](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/01/07/3214475/0/en/What-Will-Good-Hiring-Look-Like-in-2026-New-Employ-Inc-Report-Reveals-Key-Recruiting-Benchmarks.html), it fell from 23.7% in 2024 to 12.1% in 2025. Good documentation doesn't just help you remember. It protects your organization legally, reduces bias, and gives your hiring team a shared factual basis for every decision.

This guide covers the how-to of effective note-taking, three templates you can use immediately, a filled-in example, EEOC compliance rules, and the mistakes that quietly undermine most interview documentation.

But that predictive power depends on accurate documentation. An interview without notes is just a conversation with no audit trail.

## How Should You Take Interview Notes?

To write interview notes that hold up, prepare a structured template before the interview, capture behaviors and direct quotes during it, and expand everything within 15 minutes after. The difference between useful documentation and a scribbled mess comes down to preparation, because you can't record an answer well while you're still deciding what to write. Here's the process that works, and it's the same one to follow if you're learning how to write interview notes and scorecards together.

### Before the Interview

**Prepare a structured template.** Decide which competencies you're evaluating before the interview starts - not during it. Map each competency to 1-2 questions and build a scoring rubric. (Three ready-to-use templates are included later in this article.)

**Review the candidate's resume.** Note job-relevant observations only. Don't annotate the resume itself - write on a separate evaluation form. Writing directly on a resume creates a discoverable document that might contain impressions unrelated to job qualifications.

**Set up your workspace.** Whether you're using a laptop, tablet, or paper form, have your template ready and accessible. Using a shared scoring system? Confirm you have access before the candidate arrives.

### During the Interview

**Tell the candidate you'll be taking notes.** A simple "I'll be jotting things down so I can accurately capture your responses" sets expectations and signals professionalism. Most candidates actually appreciate it - it shows you're paying attention.

**Capture behaviors, not impressions.** Write what the candidate said and did, not how they made you feel. "Described leading a 4-person team through a system migration in 3 weeks" is useful. "Seemed confident" is not.

**Use the STAR method as a capture framework.** When a candidate answers a behavioral question, note the Situation they described, the Task they faced, the Action they took, and the Result they achieved. This structure makes notes consistent across candidates and interviewers. For more on behavioral interviewing frameworks, see our guide to [structured interviews](https://www.pin.com/blog/structured-interviews-guide/).

**Write in shorthand.** You don't need complete sentences. Abbreviations, keywords, and fragments are fine as long as you'll understand them 15 minutes later when you review. The goal is staying present in the conversation while capturing enough to reconstruct the answer later.

**Score as you go.** If your template includes a rating scale, fill it in after each question while the answer is fresh. Waiting until the end introduces recency bias - you'll over-weight the last few answers.

### After the Interview

**Expand your notes within 15 minutes.** Short-term memory degrades rapidly, and unrehearsed details start fading within seconds. Block 10-15 minutes after each interview to flesh out abbreviations, complete partial notes, and finalize scores while the conversation is still vivid.

**Transfer to your system of record.** Move your notes into your ATS, HRIS, or shared evaluation platform. Don't leave notes in personal notebooks, loose paper, or private email threads. Centralized storage makes notes accessible to the hiring team and discoverable for compliance.

## Which Interview Note Template Should You Use?

Use a basic interview notes form for screening calls, a STAR template for behavioral interviews, and a competency-based rubric for panels and final rounds. Each one is a copy-ready interview notes format: paste it into a doc, a shared form, or your ATS scorecard, and it solves the "what should I write down?" problem before the candidate joins. Pick the one that matches your process, or combine elements from several.

### Template 1: Basic Interview Notes Form

Best for: [screening interviews](/blog/screening-interview-guide/), first-round calls, and teams that are just starting to formalize their process. If you only adopt one interview note taking template, make it this one, since it works for almost any role.

| Field | What to Write |
| --- | --- |
| <strong>Candidate Name</strong> | [Full name] |
| <strong>Position</strong> | [Role title and req number] |
| <strong>Interviewer</strong> | [Your name] |
| <strong>Date / Time</strong> | [Interview date and time] |
| <strong>Q1: [Question text]</strong> | [Candidate's response - key points, direct quotes, examples given] |
| <strong>Q2: [Question text]</strong> | [Candidate's response] |
| <strong>Q3: [Question text]</strong> | [Candidate's response] |
| <strong>Technical/Role Skills</strong> | [Relevant skills demonstrated or mentioned] |
| <strong>Questions Candidate Asked</strong> | [What they wanted to know - signals priorities and engagement] |
| <strong>Overall Rating</strong> | 1 (No hire) / 2 (Below bar) / 3 (At bar) / 4 (Above bar) / 5 (Strong hire) |
| <strong>Advance to Next Round?</strong> | Yes / No / Maybe - with brief justification |

### Template 2: STAR Method Template

Best for: behavioral interviews, mid-to-senior roles, and any position where past performance is the strongest predictor of future success. It's the most detailed job interview notes template of the three.

| Competency | Question | Situation | Task | Action | Result | Score (1-5) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Leadership | "Tell me about a time you led a team through a difficult project." | [Context they described] | [Their specific responsibility] | [What they personally did] | [Measurable outcome] | [1-5] |
| Problem-Solving | "Describe a situation where you had to solve a problem with limited information." | [Context] | [Responsibility] | [Actions taken] | [Outcome] | [1-5] |
| Collaboration | "Give an example of working with a difficult colleague." | [Context] | [Responsibility] | [Actions taken] | [Outcome] | [1-5] |
| [Role-Specific] | "[Custom question]" | [Context] | [Responsibility] | [Actions taken] | [Outcome] | [1-5] |

**Scoring guide:** 1 = No relevant example given. 2 = Vague example, missing key STAR elements. 3 = Solid example with all STAR elements. 4 = Strong example with measurable results. 5 = Exceptional example showing initiative, impact, and learning.

### Template 3: Competency-Based Scoring Rubric

Best for: panel interviews, final rounds, and organizations that need consistent cross-interviewer calibration. This template uses behaviorally anchored rating scales (BARS), which research shows reduce scoring bias and increase reliability.

| Competency | 1 - Does Not Meet | 2 - Partially Meets | 3 - Meets | 4 - Exceeds | 5 - Far Exceeds | Score |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <strong>Technical Skills</strong> | Cannot describe relevant tools or methods | Basic familiarity, no hands-on examples | Demonstrated proficiency with concrete examples | Advanced usage with process improvements | Expert-level mastery driving business impact | [1-5] |
| <strong>Communication</strong> | Unclear, disorganized responses | Understandable but lacks structure | Clear and organized answers | Articulate with strong examples | Compelling communicator who adapts to audience | [1-5] |
| <strong>Problem-Solving</strong> | Cannot articulate approach to problems | Generic approach, no specifics | Logical process with a relevant example | Creative solutions with measurable outcomes | Systematic thinker who anticipates downstream effects | [1-5] |
| <strong>Culture Add</strong> | Values misaligned with team needs | Some alignment, limited evidence | Values and work style match team dynamics | Brings complementary perspective | Would meaningfully strengthen team diversity of thought | [1-5] |

**How to use this rubric:** Before the interview, define which competencies matter for the role and customize the behavioral anchors. During the interview, circle or highlight the descriptor that best matches the candidate's demonstrated level. After the interview, compare scores across interviewers during a [calibration session](/blog/interview-debrief-guide/).

## What Does a Good Interview Notes Example Look Like?

A good interview notes example records what the candidate actually said, ties it to one competency, and attaches a score, so a colleague who missed the conversation could reach the same conclusion. Below are sample interview notes on a candidate for a customer success manager role, written with the STAR template. The left column is what most interviewers jot down. The right column is what survives a debrief, a second-round handoff, or an EEOC review.

| Competency | Weak note | Strong note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Problem-solving | "Good answer, seemed sharp." | S: churn spike in a 40-account book. T: stop it before renewals. A: built a health-score sheet, called 12 at-risk accounts in 2 weeks. R: 10 of 12 renewed. Score: 4 |
| Communication | "Great communicator." | Walked an upset customer through a price change. Quote: "I told them what changed, why, and what it meant for their invoice." Score: 3 |
| Collaboration | "Nice personality, team player." | Fixed an onboarding bug with product through the weekly triage. Could not name a result when asked. Score: 2 |

Notice what the strong column leaves out: adjectives about personality, guesses about motivation, and anything tied to a protected characteristic. Every line is an observation first and a score second.

Close each set of notes with a three-line interview summary. It's the part a hiring manager reads first, and it keeps your recommendation tied to the evidence above it:

-   **Overall:** Advance to the panel round (average score 3.0 across three competencies).
-   **Strongest evidence:** Renewed 10 of 12 at-risk accounts after building a health-score sheet.
-   **Open question:** The collaboration story had no outcome, so probe cross-functional work in round two.

That summary block works as an interview summary template for any role. Swap in your own competencies, keep the three labels, and your debriefs start from the same facts.

## What Are the EEOC Requirements for Interview Notes?

The EEOC requires private employers to keep interview notes for at least one year, to keep protected characteristics out of them, and to preserve them with no end date once a discrimination charge is filed. That makes your notes legal documents, not just an internal tool. The [EEOC](https://www.eeoc.gov/employers/recordkeeping-requirements) spells out what must be retained and for how long, and getting it wrong exposes your organization to discrimination claims.

### Record Retention Requirements

| Employer Type | Minimum Retention Period | Authority |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Private employers (15+ employees) | 1 year from the record or hiring decision, whichever is later | EEOC - 29 CFR 1602.14 |
| Federal contractors (150+ employees and a $150K+ contract) | 2 years (1 year below either threshold) | OFCCP - 41 CFR 60-1.12 |
| School systems and state/local governments | 2 years | EEOC - 29 CFR 1602.31 and 1602.40 |
| Any employer (when a charge is filed) | Until final disposition of the charge - no time limit | EEOC - 29 CFR Part 1602 |

That last row is the one most employers miss. Once a discrimination charge is filed, there's no expiration on your retention obligation. Every piece of hiring documentation related to that charge must be preserved until the matter is fully resolved - whether that takes months or years.

### What Must Never Appear in Interview Notes

The [EEOC prohibits](https://www.eeoc.gov/employers/small-business/what-shouldnt-i-ask-when-hiring) employment decisions based on protected characteristics. If those characteristics show up in your interview notes, they become evidence of potential discriminatory intent - even if you didn't consciously factor them into your decision. The scale of this problem is larger than most employers realize: 53% of US job seekers report facing illegal or discriminatory questions during interviews, according to [Greenhouse's 2025 Workforce & Hiring Report](https://www.greenhouse.com/blog/greenhouse-2025-workforce-hiring-report) (2,200 job seekers across the US, UK, and Ireland). That means the risk isn't just theoretical, and your documentation is the clearest record of what was and wasn't asked.

**Never document:**

-   Race, color, or national origin
-   Religion or religious practices
-   Sex, pregnancy, or family planning status
-   Age (unless legally required for the position)
-   Disability status (pre-offer ADA protections are strict)
-   Genetic information or family medical history
-   Physical appearance unrelated to job requirements
-   Club or organization memberships that could reveal protected characteristics

**What to document instead:** Job-relevant behaviors, specific answers, demonstrated skills, technical knowledge, and measurable accomplishments. If you can't tie your note to a job qualification, don't write it down.

### Safe Documentation Practices

Write on evaluation forms, never directly on resumes or applications. Store notes in your ATS or HRIS - not in personal email, Slack messages, or paper files that might get lost. Restrict access to interview notes to people directly involved in the hiring decision.

## What Are the Best Practices for Interview Documentation?

The best practices for interview documentation come down to seven habits. Keep one template per role, quote candidates directly, record observations before scores, score after each question, write up within 15 minutes, skip personal impressions, and calibrate before the debrief. None of them will fix every hiring miss. Together, though, they close the gap between what interviewers observed and what the hiring team actually uses to decide.

1.  **Use the same template for every candidate in the same role.** Consistency is the foundation. When different interviewers use different formats - or no format at all - you can't compare candidates apples to apples. Standardized templates are how [a structured interview framework](https://www.pin.com/blog/structured-interviews-guide/) delivers its predictive advantage.
2.  **Document direct quotes.** "I reduced onboarding time from 3 weeks to 5 days by building an automated training module" is infinitely more useful than "good answer about onboarding." Quotes ground your notes in specifics and make calibration discussions productive.
3.  **Separate observations from evaluations.** First write what happened. Then score it. Blending the two leads to confirmation bias - you start writing notes that support the rating you've already assigned instead of letting the evidence drive the score.
4.  **Score after each question, not at the end.** Recency bias is real. If you wait until the interview is over to assign scores, the last 10 minutes will dominate your assessment while early strong answers fade. Rate each competency immediately after the relevant question.
5.  **Expand notes within 15 minutes.** Memory degrades fast, and short-term recall drops sharply within seconds without reinforcement. That 15-minute window after the interview is when your abbreviations still make sense and the candidate's tone and emphasis are still fresh. Block it on your calendar.
6.  **Skip personal impressions.** "Nice personality" and "good energy" aren't job qualifications. They also introduce exactly the kind of subjective bias that structured notes are designed to eliminate. If you can't connect an observation to a specific competency on your scorecard, leave it out.
7.  **Calibrate with other interviewers before debriefing.** If multiple people interviewed the same candidate, compare scores and notes before discussing impressions. This prevents anchoring - where one interviewer's strong opinion pulls the group in their direction regardless of what the evidence shows.

## What Mistakes Should You Avoid in Interview Notes?

The costliest note-taking mistakes are writing everything after the interview, annotating the resume, and using vague labels where evidence should be. Each one leaves a gap that a hiring committee fills with memory and opinion, which is exactly what structured documentation exists to prevent. Here are the mistakes to watch for.

**Writing notes after the interview.** Waiting even an hour means you're reconstructing the conversation from fading memory rather than documenting what actually happened. Always write during the interview, then expand immediately after.

**Taking notes on the resume.** It's tempting to scribble in the margins, but annotated resumes become discoverable legal documents. If your notes include anything related to a protected characteristic - even an innocent observation about accent or appearance - they can be used as evidence. Always use a separate evaluation form.

**Documenting only positive or only negative points.** Cherry-picking evidence to support a predetermined conclusion is called confirmation bias, and it defeats the entire purpose of structured documentation. Capture the full picture - strong answers and weak ones.

**Using generic descriptors instead of specifics.** "Great communicator" tells a hiring committee nothing. "Explained a complex API migration to non-technical stakeholders using a simple analogy, then fielded 4 follow-up questions clearly" tells them everything. Always choose specifics over labels.

**Neglecting to store notes properly.** Handwritten notes stuffed in a desk drawer can't be searched, shared, or produced during an EEOC investigation. Always transfer notes to your centralized system within 24 hours.

**Inconsistent scoring across interviewers.** If one interviewer uses a 1-5 scale and another writes paragraph summaries with no rating, you can't aggregate results. Align on a shared rubric before interviews start, and calibrate regularly. ## How Is AI Changing Interview Documentation?

AI is moving interview documentation from handwritten notes to real-time transcripts and auto-filled scorecards, but it works best as a backup to human judgment rather than a replacement for it. Since a third of employers still don't run structured interviews at all, there's clear room for these tools to close that gap. The current generation transcribes interviews live, flags potential bias in notes, and drafts scoring rubrics from candidate responses.

The benefits are clear. AI note-taking eliminates the split-attention problem where interviewers try to listen and write simultaneously. It captures exact quotes instead of paraphrased approximations. And it creates a searchable record that's immediately available to the entire hiring team.

But there are tradeoffs. Automated transcription raises privacy and consent questions - some states require all-party consent for [recording job interviews](/blog/why-record-interviews/). AI-generated summaries can miss nuance and context that [human interviewers](/blog/good-interviewer-bad-interviewer/) pick up on. And over-reliance on AI notes can reduce interviewer engagement if they stop listening carefully because "the tool is capturing it."

The practical approach? Use AI transcription as a backup and reference tool, not a replacement for human observation. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are interview notes?

Interview notes are the written record an interviewer keeps of a candidate's answers, examples, and scores during a job interview. Useful ones capture direct quotes and observable behaviors tied to the role's competencies, not personal impressions. They feed the hiring debrief, let interviewers compare candidates on the same criteria, and become the employer's record if a hiring decision is ever challenged.

### How do you write interview notes?

Start with a template that lists the competencies and questions for the role. During the interview, jot shorthand on what the candidate said and did, use the STAR framework for behavioral answers, and score each competency on a shared [interview scorecard](/blog/interview-scorecards-templates/) right after the relevant question. Within 15 minutes of finishing, expand your abbreviations and add a three-line summary. Aim for 200-400 words per interview, which is enough for specific evidence without transcribing everything.

### What are best practices for managing notes from interviews or candidate calls?

Keep every note in one system of record, usually your ATS, rather than in notebooks, personal docs, or chat threads. Use the same template for everyone interviewing for the same role, restrict access to the people involved in the decision, and transfer handwritten notes within 24 hours. ### How long do I need to keep interview notes on file?

Private employers must retain all hiring records, including your interview documentation, for at least 1 year from the date the record was made or the hiring decision, whichever is later, per [EEOC regulations](https://www.eeoc.gov/employers/recordkeeping-requirements) (29 CFR 1602.14). Federal contractors with 150+ employees and a government contract of $150,000 or more must keep them for 2 years. If a discrimination charge is filed, retain all related records until the charge reaches final disposition, with no time limit.

### Can interview notes be used as evidence in a discrimination lawsuit?

Yes. Interview notes are discoverable documents in EEOC investigations and lawsuits. This is exactly why notes should never reference protected characteristics like age, race, gender, religion, disability, or family status. Document only job-relevant behaviors, skills, and answers. Notes that contain subjective impressions about appearance or personality can be interpreted as evidence of bias.
