Reference check best practices exist to protect one of the most consequential decisions a recruiter makes: verifying a candidate's qualifications, work history, and on-the-job behavior before extending a final offer. In a [2025 Checkr survey of 3,000 U.S. managers](https://checkr.com/resources/articles/hiring-hoax-manager-survey-2025), 31% said they had interviewed a candidate later found to be using a fake identity. That makes verification a core step of the [hiring process](https://www.pin.com/blog/recruitment-funnel-benchmarks/). Skipping it is expensive: once recruiting, hiring, and onboarding are counted, a bad hire can cost as much as $240,000, according to an estimate [quoted by SHRM](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/employee-relations/cost-bad-hire-can-astronomical).

Yet most recruiters treat reference checks as a checkbox exercise. They call a couple of names on a list, ask vague questions, and move on. That approach misses the entire point. A well-run reference check reveals patterns that resumes, interviews, and even skills assessments can't surface. These include how a candidate handles conflict, whether they actually did what they claim, and whether their former manager would hire them again.

What follows are the reference check best practices you need: exactly what to ask, when to ask it, and how to run a process that actually protects hiring decisions.

> **TL;DR:**
>
> - **Reference checks still catch what interviews miss.** [OPM](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/assessment-and-selection/other-assessment-methods/reference-checking/) finds they predict job performance better than years of education or job experience, and a bad hire can cost up to $240,000.
> - **"Would you rehire this person?" is the killer question.** Any answer short of an immediate "yes" deserves a follow-up, which makes it the strongest single signal in most reference calls.
> - **Standardize across 8 categories and 3+ references.** Use the same rubric for every candidate to compare apples to apples and reduce bias.
> - **Time them post-offer.** Checking references after a conditional offer reduces discrimination risk, respects the candidate's current employer, and filters out anyone who won't reach the offer stage.
> - **AI-era fraud makes this non-negotiable.** 31% of managers involved in hiring have interviewed candidates using fake identities and 59% suspect AI-generated misrepresentation ([Checkr, 2025](https://checkr.com/resources/articles/hiring-hoax-manager-survey-2025)), so human-verified references are a real safeguard.

## What Is a Reference Check and Why Does It Still Matter?

A reference check is a structured conversation with someone who has directly supervised, managed, or worked closely with a job candidate. Its purpose: verify employment history, assess job performance, and uncover behavioral patterns that applicants won't volunteer during interviews.

Some recruiters question whether reference checks are worth the effort in 2026. Yes - and the data backs it up clearly. The [2025 Checkr survey](https://checkr.com/resources/articles/hiring-hoax-manager-survey-2025) found that 31% of managers had personally interviewed a candidate who turned out to be using a fake identity. Even more, 59%, suspected a candidate of using AI to misrepresent their qualifications.

Resume fraud isn't new, but AI tools have made it easier to fabricate work histories, inflate titles, and even generate fake references. In this environment, reference checks serve as one of the few verification steps that involve a real human confirming real facts.

In practice, experienced agency recruiters report that the reference call is often where the real story emerges. A candidate might interview beautifully but have a manager who hesitates when asked about reliability. That hesitation tells you more than any assessment score. The reference check is where gut feelings get validated - or contradicted - by people who've actually managed the candidate's day-to-day work.

Reference checks also matter legally. In 2024, a [Texas jury awarded more than $60 million](https://coverlink.com/case-study/nuclear-verdict-case-study-60-million-negligent-hiring-retention-supervision-verdict/) in a negligent hiring case over a 2018 assault by a Texas Live! security guard who had a prior assault conviction. Skipping reference checks doesn't just risk a bad hire - it creates legal exposure.

Having built Interseller - a recruiting outreach platform serving thousands of staffing agencies - we watched reference-check habits evolve as AI sourcing changed what recruiters knew about candidates before picking up the phone. One pattern stood out: teams that skipped checks on speed grounds got burned not on technical skills but on collaboration style. Applicants who delivered results but destroyed team morale are invisible in a 45-minute interview. Those calls surface the pattern.

Pin's 2026 user survey shows 83% of candidates Pin recommends are accepted into hiring pipelines. Strong AI matching drives that rate - but even then, customers consistently report that reference checks convert a "probably good" into a "definitely yes," or catch the red flag they almost missed. Strong sourcing and verified references are complementary, not redundant. Betting only on the AI means missing what a former manager will say between the lines.

## When Should You Conduct Reference Checks?

Conduct reference checks after extending a conditional offer. That timing protects both parties and produces the most useful information. Run them too early, and you waste time on candidates who won't make the shortlist. Too late, and you've already emotionally committed before validating the decision.

Post-offer, pre-start-date is the industry standard for reference timing. Three advantages make this the default:

-   **Legal protection** - Checking references post-offer reduces the risk of discrimination claims, especially in jurisdictions with ban-the-box laws (37 states plus DC have adopted fair-chance hiring policies, per the [National Employment Law Project](https://www.nelp.org/insights-research/ban-the-box-fair-chance-hiring-state-and-local-guide/)).
-   **Efficiency** - You're only investing reference check time on candidates you've already vetted through [a rubric-based interview process](https://www.pin.com/blog/structured-interviews-guide/), skills assessments, and team evaluations.
-   **Candidate cooperation** - Candidates are more willing to provide reference contact details after receiving an offer, and references are more forthcoming when they know the candidate is a finalist.

For executive or senior roles, consider running preliminary reference checks earlier - during the final interview round - since these positions carry higher risk and the reference pool is often smaller.

Manual reference checks typically take 3 to 7 business days. Automated reference checking platforms can cut that to 24-48 hours, though verbal follow-ups are still recommended for critical roles.

## What Reference Check Questions Should You Ask?

The best reference check questions cover 8 categories to build a complete picture: employment verification, performance, strengths, development areas, work style, reliability, rehire eligibility, and open-ended probes. This structure gives complete coverage without redundant questions, and using the same set for every candidate strengthens legal defensibility.

Here are 23 questions across 8 categories, plus 3 for personal references, adapted from common recruiter frameworks such as [LinkedIn's Talent Blog](https://www.linkedin.com/business/talent/blog/talent-acquisition/reference-check-questions).

### 1\. Employment Verification

Start here. These questions confirm basic facts and establish whether the reference is legitimate.

-   Can you confirm \[candidate's name\] worked at your organization from \[start date\] to \[end date\]?
-   What was their job title and what were their primary responsibilities?
-   Did their role change during their time with you? If so, how?

### 2\. Performance and Accomplishments

These questions reveal whether the candidate delivered results or simply occupied a seat.

-   What were this person's most significant accomplishments in your organization?
-   How would you rate their overall job performance on a scale of 1 to 10?
-   Did they meet or exceed the goals set for their role?

### 3\. Strengths and Development Areas

Good references will give you honest, specific answers here. Vague responses are a signal worth noting.

-   What are this person's greatest professional strengths?
-   What areas would you suggest they focus on for professional development?
-   How did they handle constructive feedback?

### 4\. Work Style and Relationships

These questions map to culture fit and team dynamics - critical for predicting how a candidate will perform in your specific environment.

-   Does this person work better independently or as part of a team?
-   How would you describe their communication style with colleagues?
-   How did they handle disagreements or conflict within the team?

### 5\. Reliability and Professionalism

-   How would you rate their dependability and punctuality?
-   Did they ever receive any formal recognition, awards, or promotions?
-   How did they perform under pressure or tight deadlines?

### 6\. The Rehire Question

Of all the reference check questions you can ask, the rehire question is the hardest for a reference to dodge gracefully. Any answer other than an immediate, enthusiastic "yes" is a red flag worth investigating further. Recruiters who've run thousands of these calls consistently report that the rehire question surfaces more honest signal than any other single question in the interview.

-   Would you rehire this person if given the opportunity? Why or why not?
-   If you could change one thing about their performance or behavior, what would it be?

### 7\. Reason for Departure

-   Why did this person leave your organization?
-   Was their departure voluntary or involuntary?
-   Did you attempt to retain them? If so, what happened?

### 8\. Open-Ended Probes

These catch anything the structured questions missed. Let the reference talk - silence after these questions often produces the most valuable information.

-   Is there anything else I should know before making a hiring decision about this person?
-   What type of role or environment do you think they'd thrive in most?
-   How would you compare this person to others you've managed in a similar role?

### Questions for Personal or Character References

Personal references can't speak to job performance, so don't ask them to. When a candidate has a thin work history (a new graduate, a career changer, someone returning after a long gap), a character reference still tells you something about integrity and follow-through. Keep these calls short and anchored in specifics:

-   How long have you known [candidate's name], and in what context?
-   Can you describe a time they took on a commitment and saw it through?
-   How do they react when plans fall apart or something goes wrong?

Weight these answers below anything a former supervisor tells you. They supplement professional reference check questions; they never replace them.

### Step 1: Get Written Consent

Before contacting any reference, obtain the candidate's written authorization. This isn't just courtesy - it's a legal requirement in many jurisdictions, and the [EEOC](https://www.eeoc.gov/laws/guidance/background-checks-what-employers-need-know) expects documented consent for any pre-employment inquiries. Include a consent clause in your application or offer letter that specifically authorizes reference checks.

### Step 2: Request at Least 3 References

Ask for a minimum of three references: at least two direct supervisors and one peer or cross-functional colleague. If the candidate is applying for a management role, request at least one direct report as well. Avoid accepting only personal or character references - you need people who observed the candidate's work firsthand.

### Step 3: Verify the Reference's Identity

Don't just call the number the candidate gave you. Look up the reference's name on LinkedIn, verify they actually worked at the company in the timeframe the candidate claims, and call the company's main number to confirm the reference's role. Fake references get caught here - and [62% of managers](https://checkr.com/resources/articles/hiring-hoax-manager-survey-2025) in Checkr's 2025 survey believe job seekers are now better at faking identities than hiring teams are at detecting them.

### Step 4: Use Standardized Questions

Use the same set of questions for every reference check on every candidate for the same role. Consistent questioning protects against disparate impact claims and produces comparable data for real evaluation. Pick 8-12 questions from the categories above and use them as your standard reference check template.

### Step 5: Conduct Verbal Follow-Ups

Even if you use an automated reference checking platform, conduct at least two phone or video calls with references for each finalist candidate. Automated tools are excellent at catching fraud and collecting standardized responses quickly, but human conversation reveals nuances that written questionnaires miss - tone of voice, hesitations, and the things a reference says between the lines.

### Step 6: Document Everything

Record the date, time, reference name, their relationship to the candidate, and detailed notes from every conversation. Your documentation is your defense when a hiring decision is challenged. Keep reference check records for at least one year, or longer if required by your state's employment laws.

### Step 7: Share Findings with the Hiring Manager

Compile reference check results into a structured summary and share it with the [hiring manager](https://www.pin.com/blog/hiring-manager-collaboration/) before the final decision. Don't just forward raw notes - highlight themes, flag inconsistencies, and note whether the reference information matches what the candidate presented during their [formal interview rounds](https://www.pin.com/blog/structured-interviews-guide/).

**1\. The reference can't confirm basic facts.** If a reference can't verify the candidate's title, dates of employment, or general responsibilities, that's a significant concern. Either the candidate misrepresented their history or the reference isn't who they claim to be.

**2\. Vague, rehearsed, or extremely brief answers.** One-word answers or responses that sound scripted suggest the reference is either hiding something or was coached by the candidate. "They were fine" isn't a reference - it's a deflection.

**3\. The reference is a peer or friend, not a supervisor.** Candidates sometimes list friends or peers as references to avoid supervisor scrutiny. If every reference is a colleague at the same level rather than a direct manager, ask why no supervisors are on the list.

**4\. Hesitation on the rehire question.** When you ask "Would you rehire this person?" the answer should be immediate. Any pause, hedge, or qualified response ("Well, it would depend on the role...") typically signals a problem the reference doesn't want to articulate directly.

**5\. Multiple references use identical language.** If two or more references give suspiciously similar answers - same phrases, same examples, same structure - the candidate may have coached them or provided scripted talking points. Genuine references describe the same person differently because they experienced the person from different angles.

**6\. The reference's contact details don't check out.** Phone numbers that route to a personal cell instead of a business line are suspicious. So are email addresses on free domains rather than corporate ones, or LinkedIn profiles that don't match the reference's claimed role. Cross-check every reference through the company's public contact information.

## What Can You Legally Ask in a Reference Check?

Employment dates, job performance, rehire eligibility, and reason for leaving are all fair game. Protected class information, medical history, and salary history are not - EEOC complaints, state-level penalties, or lawsuits follow when recruiters get this wrong. [EEOC guidance](https://www.eeoc.gov/laws/guidance/background-checks-what-employers-need-know) sets the baseline; state rules add further constraints that vary by jurisdiction.


### What You Can Ask

-   **Employment dates and job title** - Factual, verifiable, and legally safe.
-   **Job responsibilities and duties** - Directly relevant to evaluating the candidate's qualifications.
-   **Job performance and accomplishments** - Fair game when the reference is a supervisor who observed work directly.
-   **Eligibility for rehire** - Most companies will answer this yes or no, even if they won't elaborate.
-   **Reason for leaving** - Permitted in most jurisdictions, though some references will only confirm voluntary vs. involuntary.
-   **Work style, communication, and teamwork** - Behavioral questions tied to job requirements are acceptable.

### What You Cannot Ask

The [EEOC](https://www.eeoc.gov/laws/guidance/background-checks-what-employers-need-know) is explicit: employers cannot gather or use information based on race, color, national origin, sex (including gender identity and pregnancy), religion, disability, genetic information, or age (40+). During reference checks, this means:

-   **No questions about medical conditions or disabilities** - Not before or after a conditional offer. Period.
-   **No questions about family status or pregnancy** - "Is she planning to have children?" is illegal.
-   **No questions about age or retirement plans** - "How close is he to retirement?" violates the ADEA.
-   **No questions about religion, national origin, or citizenship** - Focus solely on ability to perform the job.
-   **No salary history inquiries** - Banned statewide in 22 states, per [HR Dive's tracker](https://www.hrdive.com/news/salary-history-ban-states-list/516662/), plus dozens of cities and counties.
-   **No blanket criminal history disqualification** - The EEOC requires individualized assessment of criminal records, not automatic rejection.

### Qualified Privilege for Reference Providers

If you're giving a reference (not just requesting one), know that most U.S. states recognize "qualified privilege" - a legal protection for employers who provide honest, good-faith reference information about a former employee's job performance. This privilege protects you from defamation claims, as long as the information is truthful and limited to job-related performance observations.

However, the privilege is lost if the information is knowingly false, motivated by personal malice, or extends beyond job-related facts. Some employers default to a "name, rank, and dates" policy to minimize risk, but this creates its own problem: when no one gives substantive references, bad hires circulate more freely. The better approach is training managers to give honest, factual, job-focused references rather than refusing to give them at all.

## What Does a Good Reference Check Template Look Like?

A standardized template spanning eight categories - from verification through open-ended probes - produces comparable, legally defensible data across candidates. Here's a ready-to-use version. Customize it for your industry and role level, but keep the core structure consistent across every candidate for the same position.

| Category | Question | What to Listen For |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Verification | Can you confirm [name]'s title and dates of employment? | Exact match to resume claims |
| Performance | How would you rate their overall performance (1-10)? | Specific examples, not just a number |
| Strengths | What are their greatest professional strengths? | Alignment with role requirements |
| Development | What areas could they improve? | Honesty vs. evasion |
| Work Style | Do they work better independently or on a team? | Match to your team's dynamics |
| Reliability | How did they perform under pressure? | Concrete examples of deadlines met |
| Rehire | Would you rehire this person? | Immediate yes vs. hesitation |
| Open-Ended | Anything else I should know before making a decision? | Unprompted disclosures |

Track responses in a spreadsheet or your ATS so you can compare references across candidates for the same role. Use consistent scoring (1-5 scale per question) to make the comparison objective rather than impressionistic.

Tools like interview scorecards work for reference checks too - the same principle of standardized evaluation applies. And if you want to track how reference check outcomes correlate with on-the-job performance, start measuring [quality of hire](https://www.pin.com/blog/quality-of-hire-metrics/) alongside your reference data.

## What Mistakes Undermine Reference Check Best Practices?

Five mistakes consistently undermine reference check best practices. Avoiding them won't guarantee a perfect hire, but it will dramatically improve the signal you get from every call.

**Accepting only candidate-provided references.** Candidates naturally list people who'll say positive things. Supplement their list with back-channel references - colleagues you find through LinkedIn or your own professional network who worked with the candidate but weren't hand-picked by them.

**Asking leading questions.** "She's a great team player, right?" guarantees a useless answer. Ask open-ended questions that don't telegraph the answer you're hoping to hear. "How would you describe her approach to teamwork?" produces real information.

**Rushing through the call.** A five-minute reference call is a waste of everyone's time. Budget 15-20 minutes per reference and be comfortable with silence. When a reference pauses after a question, don't fill the gap - let them think and respond fully.

**Skipping references entirely for senior candidates.** Some recruiters assume executives don't need reference checks because they have long track records. The opposite is true. Senior hires carry higher risk, higher salary costs, and wider organizational impact. If anything, they need more thorough reference checking.

**Treating reference checks as a pass/fail exercise.** Reference checks aren't meant to produce a simple hire/don't-hire verdict. They're a data source that feeds into your overall evaluation alongside [interview feedback](https://www.pin.com/blog/interview-feedback-examples/), skills assessments, and team input. Use them to build a complete picture, not to make a binary decision.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do you do a good reference check?

Get written consent, then call at least 3 references, two of whom directly supervised the candidate. Verify each reference's identity through the company's main line before the call. Ask every reference the same standardized questions, end with the rehire question, and budget 15-20 minutes so there's room for silence. Write up themes and inconsistencies for the hiring manager instead of forwarding raw notes.

### What are you legally allowed to ask in a reference check?

You can ask about employment dates, title, responsibilities, job performance, reliability, reason for leaving, and eligibility for rehire. You can't ask about anything tied to a protected characteristic under [EEOC rules](https://www.eeoc.gov/laws/guidance/background-checks-what-employers-need-know): race, religion, national origin, sex, pregnancy, disability, genetic information, or age. Medical history and family plans are off-limits, and many states also bar salary history questions. When in doubt, ask whether the question relates directly to doing the job.

### How many references should a recruiter check?

Check a minimum of 3 references per candidate - at least 2 direct supervisors and 1 peer or cross-functional colleague. For senior roles, check 4-5 including a direct report. Three is the practical floor for spotting a pattern: one glowing or lukewarm reference can be an outlier, but three that agree usually aren't.

### What is the most important reference check question?

Most experienced recruiters point to "Would you rehire this person?" because it's hard to answer evasively without the hesitation showing. Any answer other than an immediate, clear "yes" signals a concern worth investigating. Follow up with "Why or why not?" to get the full picture.

### Can a former employer give a bad reference?

Yes. Former employers are legally permitted to provide honest, job-related feedback about a former employee's performance, including negative information. Most U.S. states recognize "qualified privilege" that protects good-faith reference providers from defamation claims. The protection only fails if the information is knowingly false or motivated by malice.

### Should reference checks happen before or after a job offer?

Industry best practice is to conduct reference checks after a conditional offer. This approach protects against discrimination claims, ensures you're only investing time on finalists, and increases reference cooperation. For executive or high-risk positions, preliminary checks during the final interview stage are also acceptable.
