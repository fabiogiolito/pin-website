# Recruiter Capacity Benchmarks 2026: Reqs Per Recruiter

A typical recruiter carries 14 to 20 open requisitions, and quality starts to break in the 20 to 30 req band. Recruiter capacity benchmarks answer the question every talent leader is quietly asking: how many requisitions per recruiter is too many? Load is still climbing, too: extra-large employers saw median requisitions per recruiter jump 67% in 2026 alone, according to [SHRM's 2026 Recruiting Executives Benchmarking](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/research/recruiting-benchmarking) of more than 4,600 organizations. ATS data puts the average at 14 open reqs, up 56% in three years (Gem 2025 Recruiting Benchmarks, 2024 data). SHRM's HR Knowledge Center has long put the median at 15 to 20, with a cross-industry average of 30 to 40. Those figures do not contradict. The gap between them is the whole story. [Pin](https://www.pin.com), the highest-rated AI recruiting platform on G2 at 4.8/5, cuts manual sourcing time 90% and reclaims 12 hours a week per recruiter. Those reclaimed hours are the single biggest lever on the capacity math below.

No single source currently assembles the full picture of recruiter req load. This report covers three things: what one recruiter can actually carry, the point where that load breaks quality, and how fast recruiters fill roles. Each is broken out by company stage and industry, and a closing section turns the numbers into a recruiter capacity model you can run on your own team. One definitional note runs throughout. "Open reqs per recruiter" is a point-in-time snapshot of how many active requisitions one recruiter is working right now, the recruiter-to-requisition ratio that defines workload. "Hire velocity" is throughput, how many hires one recruiter closes per quarter. Read together, the two define capacity.

## Key Takeaways

- **The ATS benchmark is 14 open reqs per recruiter.** That figure climbed 56% in three years (Gem, 2024 data). In Pin's in-product survey of 500+ recruiters, 50.8% juggle six or more open roles right now and 17.6% juggle 16 or more.
- **The burnout threshold starts around 20 to 30 reqs.** Above that band, intake meetings get skipped, active sourcing stops, and screening goes shallow. 59.2% of recruiters say their workload got heavier this quarter (Pin in-product survey, 400+ recruiters).
- **Hire velocity recovered, but each hire costs more work.** Recruiters closed 7.3 hires per quarter in Q1 2026, up from 4.5 in early 2023. Each hire now takes 291 applications, against roughly 100 in early 2021 (Ashby, 2026).
- **Strain scales up, not down.** SHRM measured a 67% one-year jump in median reqs per recruiter at extra-large organizations. Industry shifts the math too: technology roles draw about 51% more applicants than average, while manufacturing roles draw 49% fewer.
- **AI is the only lever that moves the math without adding headcount.** Pin reduces manual sourcing time by 90% and gives each recruiter back 12 hours a week, the fastest path to lifting req capacity in 2026.

## Recruiter Capacity Benchmarks: Open Reqs Per Recruiter in 2026

The average recruiter carries 14 open reqs by ATS count, but that single figure hides a wide spread, because the most-cited benchmarks measure different things. Gem's 14 counts the reqs actually attached to a recruiter inside the applicant tracking system, across 140M+ applications and 1M hires from 2021 through 2024 (Gem 2025 Recruiting Benchmarks). Its 2026 edition, with data through May 2025, holds near that level at 13.4 open roles at a time. SHRM's figures come from its HR Knowledge Center rather than ATS logs. There, the median fluctuates between 15 and 20 open reqs, and national averages across all industries run 30 to 40 at any one time ([SHRM](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/how-many-open-reqs-house-recruiters), 2018). Owning that distinction is the difference between a real benchmark and trivia.

Recruiters describe an even wider range when you ask them directly. In Pin's in-product survey of 500+ recruiters, fielded April to September 2026, 49.2% said they are juggling one to five open roles right now. Another 33.1% carry six to 15, 12.5% carry 16 to 30, and 5.1% carry 31 or more. Nearly half sit well under the ATS average, while roughly one in six carries more than it, which is why a single number misleads both ends of the team.

For what load is actually appropriate, Dr. John Sullivan's long-standing framework remains the most useful guide. He sets target loads by role type rather than a flat average. An exempt corporate recruiter gets 25 reqs (around 8 fills a month); a high-tech exempt recruiter, 45; non-exempt high-volume hiring, 60; and executive search just 15 to 20 ([Dr. John Sullivan](https://drjohnsullivan.com/articles/determining-the-appropriate-requisition-load-for-a-recruiter/), 2002). Loads he has observed range from 4 to 135 for a single recruiter, and he has heard of loads above 500. Role complexity, not a universal number, sets the ceiling. Assigning every recruiter the same req count quietly overloads whoever holds the hardest searches, which is why these figures belong alongside [the KPIs every recruiter should be tracking](/blog/recruiter-kpis-dashboard/) rather than in isolation.

Capacity only becomes manageable once a team actually tracks it. This rundown of the HR and recruiting KPIs worth monitoring in 2026 covers where req load, time-to-fill, and hire velocity fit on the dashboard.

## The Burnout Threshold: When Req Load Breaks Recruiters

Burnout sets in just above the median, in the 20 to 30 req band. SHRM's HR Knowledge Center puts the median at 15 to 20 and the national average at 30 to 40. Sullivan's framework lands in between, at 25 for an exempt corporate recruiter, which makes 20 to 30 the band where a manageable load turns into a stretched one. Above that load, intake meetings get skipped, active sourcing stops in favor of reviewing inbound flow, and screening goes shallow. None of those failures show up in a req count. All of them show up two months later in time-to-fill and quality-of-hire. Treat 20 to 30 as a planning range drawn from those benchmarks, never a precise "studies show burnout at exactly 27 reqs" claim, because no study measures it that cleanly.

Prevalence numbers explain why this matters now. One in six recruiters, 17.6% of 500+ in Pin's in-product survey, already carries 16 or more open roles. Asked separately, 59.2% of 400+ recruiters say their workload got heavier this quarter, against just 9.0% whose load got lighter. In a LinkedIn survey reported in January 2026, 73% of recruiters said they felt unprepared to manage the pressures of their job ([HR Dive](https://www.hrdive.com/news/recruiters-increasing-their-ai-usage-as-pressure-to-hire-intensifies/809051/), 2026). Across the wider workforce, 55% of US workers reported burnout in late 2025 ([Eagle Hill Consulting](https://www.eaglehillconsulting.com/news/workforce-burnout-survey-2025/), 2025). Among recruiters specifically, 54% found the job more stressful than the year before, per Employ's survey of 1,200 TA professionals ([SHRM](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/recruiters-express-optimism-for-2025), 2025). Prevention deserves its own treatment, so for the operational playbook on [where workload tips into recruiter burnout](/blog/recruiter-burnout-prevention/), start there.

Dig past the headline and "too many reqs" turns out to be the wrong diagnosis. What recruiters face in 2026 is a three-way squeeze that built up between 2021 and 2024. Req load rose 56% (9 to 14), applications per recruiter climbed 2.7x (to 2,500 and up), and the average TA team fell 23% (31 people to 24), per Gem's 2025 benchmarks. Team cuts have since leveled off. In Pin's in-product survey of 250+ TA professionals, fielded April to September 2026, 50.4% said their team held flat over the past 12 months and 35.9% said it grew. Only 13.7% reported a smaller team. Load is now outrunning headcount, rather than headcount falling away.

Here's what surprised us when we looked at open-req load across the recruiting teams on Pin. Strain does not start with overworked solo recruiters; it compounds as teams grow. A median solo recruiter actively works a single open req at a time. Small teams of two to five sit around three per recruiter, and teams of six or more run a median of roughly six. At those larger teams, the busiest tenth of recruiters juggle 20 or more concurrently open reqs at once. (These are point-in-time snapshots of reqs being actively worked in the last six months, which run lower than the annual-load figures SHRM and Gem report.) Read one way, scaled TA functions distribute and specialize work that a solo recruiter shoulders alone. Read the other, capacity planning that assumes load is flat across a team will under-resource exactly the people carrying the heaviest concurrent queues. Either way, the conventional wisdom that startups suffer most is backwards.

## Quarterly Hire Velocity: How Fast Recruiters Actually Fill Roles

Recruiters closed 7.3 hires per quarter by Q1 2026, a clean rebound from a trough of 4.5 in early 2023, when the application surge buried them ([Ashby 2026 Talent Trends Report](https://www.ashbyhq.com/talent-trends-report/reports/2023-recruiter-productivity-trends-report), 2026). Business recruiters reached a five-year high of 5.0 hires per quarter, while technical recruiters stabilized at 3.8. On the surface, that recovery reads as good news. Read against the capacity data, it is the opposite.

Recruiters are closing more hires per quarter than at any point since the 2023 trough, yet each hire takes far more work. The average recruiter now processes 291 applications per hire, against roughly 100 in early 2021. Candidates are about 50% less likely to receive an interview than they were five years ago, per the same Ashby report. That is not a productivity win. Instead, it is higher throughput squeezed out of a far heavier workload, exactly the pattern behind the burnout numbers above. Velocity also feeds directly into spend: every quarter a req stays open carries a real price, and [the dollar cost of a slow fill](/blog/cost-per-hire-benchmarks/) is the line item most TA leaders underestimate. Throughput held. The cost of producing it did not.

## Recruiter Capacity by Company Stage

Req load per recruiter climbs with company size rather than falling, and SHRM's 2026 data shows how sharply: extra-large organizations saw a 67% jump in median requisitions per recruiter in a single year. At seed and very early stage, there often is no recruiter at all. As a common rule of thumb, founders and department heads run hiring through the first 5 to 10 hires a year, sometimes with a fractional recruiter until volume justifies a full-time hire. A first in-house recruiter usually gets approved once the plan reaches 15 or more hires a year. As companies scale, they add sourcers and coordinators to spread the load, and at enterprise scale a single business unit can carry 1,000 or more openings a year with a structured team behind it.

Conventional wisdom says early-stage recruiters are the stretched ones. Data says otherwise. Strain is a scale phenomenon. Bigger teams hand each recruiter more concurrent reqs, because growth-stage and enterprise hiring plans expand faster than headcount budgets approve new recruiters. Because only 13.7% of TA teams in Pin's survey shrank last year, that pressure comes from req volume rising faster than teams grow, not from teams disappearing.

| Company stage | Capacity signal (rule of thumb) | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| Seed / pre-Series A | 5-10 hires/year, often no dedicated recruiter | Founders + fractional recruiter |
| First in-house hire | ~15+ hires/year triggers the role | One generalist TA partner |
| Growth / scale-up | Rising reqs per recruiter; specialization begins | Recruiters + sourcers + coordinators |
| Enterprise | 1,000+ openings/year; median reqs per recruiter up 67% in one year at extra-large orgs (SHRM, 2026) | VP TA + structured pods |

Pin's own point-in-time data confirms the inversion. Across recruiting teams on the platform, median open-req load per recruiter roughly triples as teams grow. It runs from one for solo recruiters, to three for small teams, to about six for teams of six or more. Heaviest concurrent queues, 20-plus open reqs at once, sit with the top decile of recruiters at those larger teams. Read positively, scaled functions specialize the work; read as a warning, they also concentrate it. Set alongside [the full set of sourcing and funnel benchmarks](/blog/sourcing-benchmarks-report/), this point-in-time view runs lower than annual-load figures because it counts only reqs actively worked, not everything assigned across a year.

## Recruiter Capacity by Industry

Industry changes the capacity math more than stage does, and the hidden variable is applicants per role. SmartRecruiters' Recruiting Benchmarks 2025 report draws on 89 million applications from September 2023 to August 2024. It compares five industries against a global average of 73 applicants per role and a 38-day median time to hire. The same req count means a completely different workload depending on how much applicant flow each role generates.

- **Technology**: about 51% more applicants per role than the global average, and a 48-day median time to hire, 10 days slower than the global median. Recruiters close around 26 hires a month, below the 30-hire global average.
- **Hospitality**: the heaviest applicant flow of the five, about 60% above average, with a lower hires-per-recruiter rate because many hospitality employers hire without dedicated recruiters.
- **Healthcare**: 45% fewer applicants per role, yet the heaviest recruiter workloads of any industry, at roughly 38 hires per recruiter per month and a 41-day median time to hire.
- **Manufacturing**: 49% fewer applicants per role and about 28 hires per recruiter per month, but the slowest median time to hire at 55 days, since specialized skill sets are hard to find.
- **Retail**: around 65 applicants per role and the fastest median time to hire, 25 days, or 13 days quicker than the global median.

## How to Build a Recruiter Capacity Model

A recruiter capacity model turns recruiter capacity benchmarks into a headcount number. It sizes a TA team from two inputs, open reqs and planned hires, then staffs to whichever answer demands more recruiters. Run it in three steps:

1. **Set a target load by role type.** Start from Sullivan's framework: 25 open reqs for exempt corporate roles, 45 for high-tech exempt, and 60 for high-volume non-exempt hiring, where each req is lighter to process.
2. **Adjust for the work each req demands.** Lower the target where roles draw far more applicants than the 73-applicant global average, or where thin talent pools force outbound sourcing, the pattern that stretches manufacturing's median to 55 days.
3. **Check it against throughput.** Divide planned hires per quarter by a current velocity benchmark such as Ashby's 7.3 hires per recruiter per quarter. Sullivan's 2002 framework assumed about eight fills a month, so lean on the newer figure.

Here is how that plays out. A team with 120 open exempt corporate reqs at a target of 25 needs about five recruiters. If the same team plans 60 hires this quarter, 60 divided by 7.3 calls for roughly eight. Throughput wins, so the plan is eight recruiters, or fewer if automation absorbs the sourcing work. Capacity planning usually sits with the team that owns hiring infrastructure, which is why it is one of the core pillars of [recruitment operations](/blog/recruitment-operations/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many requisitions should a recruiter have in 2026?

Most corporate recruiters should carry roughly 15 to 25 open reqs. SHRM's HR Knowledge Center puts the median at 15 to 20 and the national average at 30 to 40 ([SHRM](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/how-many-open-reqs-house-recruiters), 2018), while Dr. John Sullivan's framework sets 25 for an exempt corporate recruiter. The right number depends on role complexity: executive search caps near 15 to 20 reqs, while high-volume non-exempt hiring can sustain 60. In practice, 50.8% of recruiters in Pin's 2026 in-product survey juggle six or more open roles.

### What is a healthy req load before recruiters burn out?

Quality breaks down in the 20 to 30 req band. Above it, recruiters skip intake meetings, stop active sourcing, and shorten screening, which shows up later as slower time-to-fill and weaker quality-of-hire. In Pin's in-product survey, 17.6% of 500+ recruiters already carry 16 or more open roles, and 59.2% say their workload got heavier this quarter.

### How many hires should a recruiter make per quarter?

Recruiters averaged 7.3 hires per quarter in Q1 2026, up from a trough of 4.5 in early 2023, with business recruiters at 5.0 and technical recruiters at 3.8 ([Ashby](https://www.ashbyhq.com/talent-trends-report/reports/2023-recruiter-productivity-trends-report), 2026). That velocity returned while each hire grew to require 291 applications, nearly triple the roughly 100 of early 2021, so the same output reflects a much heavier underlying load.

### How many hires does a recruiter make per month?

Hiring mix drives the answer. Ashby's 2026 data puts the average at 7.3 hires per recruiter per quarter, or roughly two to three a month, across business and technical roles. SmartRecruiters' 2025 benchmark, which spans high-volume industries like healthcare, retail, and hospitality, averages 30 hires per recruiter per month globally, from about 26 in technology to 38 in healthcare. Plan with the lower figure for professional and technical roles, and reserve the higher one for high-volume frontline hiring.

### What is a recruiter capacity model?

A recruiter capacity model estimates how many recruiters a hiring plan needs. It compares two answers: open reqs divided by a target load per recruiter, and planned quarterly hires divided by hires per recruiter per quarter. Staff to the larger answer, then lower the target load for roles with heavy applicant flow or scarce talent.

### Do startups or enterprises have higher reqs per recruiter?

Enterprises. Capacity strain scales up, not down. SHRM's 2026 benchmarking found extra-large organizations saw median requisitions per recruiter rise 67% in a single year, because hiring plans expand faster than recruiter headcount gets approved. Early-stage companies often run on founders and fractional recruiters before a first full-time hire around 15 hires per year.

### How do you increase recruiter capacity without adding headcount?

Automate the manual funnel work. Staffing recruiters spend 14.6 hours a week on candidate search alone, and Bullhorn projects AI and automation can give back up to 17 hours a week (Bullhorn, 2025). Pin customers cut manual sourcing time 90% and reclaim 12 hours a week per recruiter, which directly raises how many reqs one recruiter can carry without crossing the burnout threshold.
