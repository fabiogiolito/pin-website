Pin users who track all 12 and automate the bottlenecks those metrics reveal fill positions in an average of 14 days, about a third of SHRM's 39-day median for nonexecutive roles.

Most [in-house recruiting teams](/blog/recruiting-team-structure/) track two or three of these. They know their time-to-fill, maybe their cost-per-hire, and call it a day. That's a problem. SHRM's [2026 Recruiting Benchmarking](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/research/recruiting-benchmarking) puts the median time-to-fill for nonexecutive roles at 39 days. Its [2025 benchmarking release](https://www.shrm.org/about/press-room/shrm-releases-2025-benchmarking-reports--how-does-your-organizat) puts average nonexecutive cost-per-hire at $5,475, up from the roughly $4,700 SHRM reported in 2022. Teams that don't measure won't know if they're above or below those benchmarks until it's too late. Worse, they won't understand _why_.

This guide breaks down all 12 KPIs with current benchmarks, formulas, and specific ways to improve each one. Whether you're building a recruitment KPIs dashboard from scratch or auditing what you already [measure with analytics tools](/blog/recruitment-analytics-tools/), here's what belongs on it.

## Which KPIs Measure Recruiting Pipeline Speed?

Pipeline KPIs tell you how fast candidates move through your funnel - and where they stall. These four metrics are the foundation of any recruiting dashboard because they're measurable from day one and directly affected by process changes.

**Having built Pin,** we've seen which KPIs teams actually act on versus which ones collect dust in a quarterly report. Most teams that start with us have solid time-to-fill visibility. What they lack is almost everything downstream: quality-of-hire scores, 90-day retention data, and any pipeline velocity breakout by stage.

According to Pin's 2026 user survey, teams using Pin reduce manual sourcing time by 90%. That compression frees up recruiter capacity - and the first thing most teams do with that capacity is start tracking the metrics they'd always deprioritized. Offer acceptance rates, candidate NPS, recruiter-to-req ratios. The recruitment KPIs dashboard gets built because there's suddenly time to build it.

Pinpointing whether a time-to-fill problem starts at sourcing, screening, or scheduling determines the right fix. Without complete data across all 12 metrics, that diagnosis takes weeks of manual analysis. With the right dashboard, it takes minutes.

### 1\. Time-to-Fill

Time-to-fill measures the total days from when a job requisition opens to when a candidate accepts the offer. SHRM's [2026 Recruiting Benchmarking](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/research/recruiting-benchmarking) puts the median at 39 days for nonexecutive roles. That number hides massive variation by role type: engineering and other technical searches usually run well past the median, high-volume sales roles tend to close faster, and executive searches take longest of all.

Why does this matter? Every open day costs money. Vacancy costs add up fast when you factor in lost productivity, overtime from the team covering the gap, and delayed projects. A 39-day median across 50 annual hires means roughly 1,950 vacancy days per year - and each of those days represents revenue and output your team isn't generating. Formalizing time-to-fill targets in a [recruiting SLA with hiring managers](https://www.pin.com/blog/recruiting-team-structure/) creates shared accountability and prevents the finger-pointing that slows hiring decisions.

**Formula:** Time-to-Fill = Date offer accepted - Date job requisition approved

**How to improve it:** The biggest drag on time-to-fill isn't sourcing - it's scheduling. Automating interview scheduling and candidate outreach attacks the waiting between steps, not the steps themselves. For a detailed breakdown of time-to-fill benchmarks by industry and role, see our [complete guide to time-to-hire metrics](https://www.pin.com/blog/time-to-hire-metrics-ai/).

### 2\. Time-to-Hire

Time-to-hire is the sharper version of time-to-fill. It starts when a specific candidate enters your pipeline and ends when they accept. Because its clock starts later, time-to-hire is always shorter than time-to-fill, and the gap between the two is the diagnostic.

Time-to-hire matters because it isolates your team's decision speed. A 39-day time-to-fill with a 25-day time-to-hire means 14 days were spent just getting candidates into the pipeline. That's a sourcing problem, not an evaluation problem. Know the difference before you try to fix anything.

**Formula:** Time-to-Hire = Date offer accepted - Date candidate entered pipeline

**How to improve it:** AI sourcing tools compress the candidate discovery phase from days to hours. Pin users fill positions in an average of 14 days, and Pin reduces time-to-hire by 82% compared to traditional methods. That gap comes from automating the sourcing, outreach, and scheduling steps that eat most of the calendar time between "candidate identified" and "offer accepted."

### 3\. Source of Hire

Source of hire tracks which channels actually produce your hires - not just which ones generate the most applications. The difference is critical. According to [iHire's 2025 State of Online Recruiting report](https://www.ihire.com/resourcecenter/employer/pages/the-state-of-online-recruiting-2025), 71.3% of employers use [employee referrals](/blog/employee-referral-programs/) as a hiring source, followed by company career pages (49.5%) and LinkedIn (46.1%). But the channel that generates the most applications rarely produces the most quality hires - outbound sourced candidates consistently convert at higher rates than inbound applicants.

Employee referrals usually deliver the best mix of cost and quality, since a referral bonus replaces most ad spend. Agency recruiters can move quickly, but their fees commonly run 15-30% of first-year salary. Direct outbound sourcing sits in between: it costs recruiter time and tooling rather than a placement fee.

**Formula:** Source of Hire = (Hires from Channel / Total Hires) x 100

**How to improve it:** Shift budget from high-volume, low-conversion channels (generic job boards) toward outbound sourcing and referrals. Track cost-per-hire and quality-of-hire by source, not just volume. Platforms like [Pin's AI sourcing](https://www.pin.com/) scan 850M+ candidate profiles to find outbound matches, which is why sourced candidates convert at 5x the rate of inbound applicants.

### 4\. Applicant-to-Interview Ratio

This ratio measures how many applicants you screen before one reaches the interview stage. A healthy benchmark is 8:1 to 12:1 for most roles - meaning roughly one in ten applicants gets an interview. Ratios above 25:1 suggest your job postings are attracting the wrong candidates. Ratios below 4:1 might mean you're not generating enough top-of-funnel volume.

The ratio has gotten worse recently. [CareerPlug's 2025 Recruiting Metrics Report](https://www.careerplug.com/recruiting-metrics-and-kpis/) (10 million+ applications across 60,000+ small businesses) found the average employer now receives roughly 180 applicants per hire. Teams are drowning in applicants but not finding more qualified candidates. That's a signal to invest in better screening, not bigger job ad budgets.

**Formula:** Applicant-to-Interview Ratio = Total Applicants / Total Interviews Scheduled

**How to improve it:** Tighten job descriptions to filter out unqualified applicants earlier. Use AI screening to score and rank candidates before a human reviews them. The goal isn't fewer applicants - it's fewer unqualified applicants reaching human review.


## Which KPIs Measure Hire Quality?

Speed means nothing if you're filling seats with people who leave in three months. Quality KPIs measure whether your hires actually work out - and whether the process itself is worth going through from the candidate's perspective.

### 5\. Quality of Hire

Quality of hire is what SHRM calls the "holy grail" of recruiting metrics - and also the hardest to measure. Only 20% of organizations currently track it, according to [SHRM's 2025 Benchmarking Report](https://www.shrm.org/about/press-room/shrm-releases-2025-benchmarking-reports--how-does-your-organizat) (surveying 2,371 HR professionals). Yet 89% of talent acquisition professionals agree that measuring quality of hire will become increasingly important, per [LinkedIn's 2025 Future of Recruiting report](https://business.linkedin.com/talent-solutions/resources/future-of-recruiting). The gap between "we should measure this" and "we actually do" is enormous. The metric requires combining multiple data points over time: performance ratings at 6 and 12 months, hiring manager satisfaction, ramp-to-productivity speed, and first-year retention.

Most teams use a composite score. A common formula weights performance reviews (40%), hiring manager satisfaction (30%), and retention at one year (30%). The exact weights depend on your organization, but the principle is the same: no single data point captures quality. You need at least three inputs measured over six to twelve months.

**Formula:** Quality of Hire = (Job Performance + Hiring Manager Satisfaction + Retention Rate) / Number of Indicators

**How to improve it:** Track quality of hire by source channel. Internal moves and referrals tend to outscore generic job-board hires, because both arrive with context a resume can't show. Where you find talent matters as much as how you evaluate them. For a deeper dive into measuring and improving this metric, see our [complete guide to quality of hire](https://www.pin.com/blog/quality-of-hire-metrics/). Teams that want to build a complete measurement practice beyond individual KPIs should explore [HR analytics frameworks](https://www.pin.com/blog/talent-analytics-guide/) that connect recruiting data to business outcomes.


### 6\. Offer Acceptance Rate

Offer acceptance rate measures the percentage of job offers that candidates accept. Most teams treat 80% as the floor for a healthy rate, and strong teams push above 90%. If your acceptance rate falls below 75%, one in four offers is being declined. Each rejection costs another 2-4 weeks of pipeline time, plus the sunk cost of every interview that led to that offer.

Declining offers usually signal one of three things: compensation misalignment, a slow process that let a competing offer arrive first, or a poor candidate experience during interviews. Diagnosing which one is the problem requires combining this KPI with time-to-hire data and candidate feedback.

**Formula:** Offer Acceptance Rate = (Offers Accepted / Offers Extended) x 100

**How to improve it:** Speed is the most underrated factor. Candidates who wait weeks for an offer have more time to accept someone else's. Automate the steps between "we want to hire this person" and "here's your offer letter." Pin's automated outreach delivers 5x better response rates than industry averages - getting talent engaged faster means less time lost to competing offers.

### 7\. New Hire Retention Rate (90-Day)

The 90-day retention rate is the ultimate quality check on your hiring process. BambooHR's [2023 new-hire study](https://www.bamboohr.com/about-bamboohr/press-release/44-of-new-hires-regret-their-decision-within-a-week-bamboohr-study-finds) found that 70% of new hires decide whether a job is the right fit within their first month, and 29% know within the first week. Every early departure means paying for recruiting, onboarding, and lost productivity twice.

A healthy 90-day retention rate is 85% or higher. Anything below 80% signals a systemic problem - either your interviews aren't surfacing the right information, your job descriptions oversell the role, or your onboarding fails to deliver on promises made during the hiring process. One way to catch these gaps early is through structured 90-day check-ins that include [self-evaluation prompts](https://www.pin.com/blog/self-evaluation-examples/) - new hires who articulate their own onboarding experience surface misalignment faster than manager observation alone.

**Formula:** 90-Day Retention Rate = (New Hires Still Employed at 90 Days / Total New Hires) x 100

**How to improve it:** The fix usually isn't better interviewing. It's better expectation-setting. Make sure job descriptions, interview conversations, and onboarding materials all describe the same role. Pin's 83% candidate acceptance rate reflects AI matching that goes beyond keyword matching to assess genuine role fit, which correlates directly with retention.

### 8\. Candidate Satisfaction (NPS)

Candidate Net Promoter Score measures whether candidates - hired or not - would recommend your hiring process to others. Starred's [candidate experience benchmarks](https://www.starred.com/blog/candidate-experience-benchmarks) put the global average at +17 on a scale from -100 to +100, unchanged from the prior year. Scores above +30 put you well ahead of most employers. A negative score means detractors outnumber promoters, and candidates are likely warning peers away.

Why track this for rejected candidates? Because they talk. In specialized fields like engineering or healthcare, those conversations happen within tight professional networks. Your employer brand is shaped more by how you treat the people you don't hire than the ones you do.

**Formula:** Candidate NPS = % Promoters (9-10 rating) - % Detractors (0-6 rating)

**How to improve it:** Respond faster. Communicate at every stage. Close the loop on every candidate, even rejections. The number one candidate complaint isn't rejection itself - it's silence. Automated status updates and personalized outreach throughout the process tend to lift NPS without adding recruiter workload.

## How Do You Track Recruiting Cost and Efficiency?

These KPIs answer the question every CFO asks: "How much are we spending per hire, and are we getting enough output from the team?" If pipeline KPIs are about speed and quality KPIs are about outcomes, cost KPIs connect [recruiting performance](/blog/hiring-health-index/) to budget.

### 9\. Cost-Per-Hire

[Cost-per-hire](https://www.pin.com/blog/cost-per-hire-benchmarks/) is the total investment your organization makes to fill one position. The average for nonexecutive roles is $5,475, per [SHRM's 2025 benchmarking release](https://www.shrm.org/about/press-room/shrm-releases-2025-benchmarking-reports--how-does-your-organizat). Executive hires cost nearly 7x more at $35,879. Both numbers keep rising: SHRM's 2022 figure for an average hire was roughly $4,700.

The SHRM/ANSI formula captures both internal costs (recruiter salaries, ATS subscriptions, referral bonuses) and external costs (agency fees, job board spend, background checks). Most companies undercount because they forget to include hiring manager interview time, which can add hundreds of dollars per senior hire.

**Formula:** Cost-Per-Hire = (Internal Recruiting Costs + External Recruiting Costs) / Total Hires

**How to improve it:** The fastest lever is reducing reliance on expensive channels. Agency fees commonly run 15-30% of first-year salary. Direct outbound sourcing through AI tools costs a fraction of that. Pin starts at $99/month billed annually, with a free tier available - dramatically lower than enterprise platforms charging $10K-$35K+ per year.

Pin's multi-channel outreach delivers 5x better response rates than industry averages - [**see how it works**](https://www.pin.com/).

### 10\. Recruiter-to-Open Req Ratio

This ratio measures [how many open positions each recruiter manages](/blog/recruiter-capacity-benchmarks/) simultaneously. Loads are climbing: SHRM's [2026 Recruiting Benchmarking](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/research/recruiting-benchmarking) reports a 67% increase in median requisitions per recruiter at extra-large organizations.

The ratio matters because it's a leading indicator of every other KPI on this list. When a recruiter manages 30 reqs, something has to give. Response times slow down. Sourcing gets shallow. Candidates wait longer for feedback. You won't see the impact on time-to-fill or quality-of-hire immediately, but it's coming.

**Formula:** Recruiter-to-Req Ratio = Open Requisitions / Number of Recruiters

**How to improve it:** The answer isn't always hiring more recruiters. AI tools that automate sourcing, outreach, and scheduling let each recruiter handle more reqs without quality dropping. According to [LinkedIn's 2025 Future of Recruiting report](https://business.linkedin.com/talent-solutions/resources/future-of-recruiting), TA pros experimenting with or integrating generative AI save roughly 20% of their work week. That's about one full day, which directly translates to higher req capacity per recruiter. For more data on how AI improves recruiter output, see our guide on [AI and recruiter productivity](https://www.pin.com/blog/recruiter-productivity-ai/).


## How Do You Measure Recruiting Throughput?

Throughput KPIs connect the dots between pipeline activity and actual outcomes. They answer the question: "Given the candidates we're processing, how efficiently are we converting them into hires?"

### 11\. Interview-to-Offer Ratio

The interview-to-offer ratio measures how many interviews your team conducts before extending one offer. A common rule of thumb is 3:1, meaning three interviews for every offer extended. Anything above 4:1 suggests too many borderline candidates are reaching the interview stage. Enterprise organizations typically reach this threshold by investing in stronger pre-interview screening and structured evaluation criteria.

This ratio reveals whether your screening process is doing its job. If you're interviewing seven candidates for every one offer, the problem isn't interviewing - it's everything upstream. Your sourcing criteria are too loose, your resume screening is too permissive, or your phone screens aren't filtering aggressively enough.

**Formula:** Interview-to-Offer Ratio = Total Interviews Conducted / Total Offers Extended

**How to improve it:** Better screening before the interview stage is the only sustainable fix. AI-powered candidate matching evaluates skills, experience, and role fit before a recruiter invests time in an interview. That's why 83% of candidates Pin recommends are accepted into customers' hiring pipelines - the AI does the heavy filtering so interviewers spend time on talent that's genuinely viable.

### 12\. Pipeline Velocity

Pipeline velocity measures how quickly candidates move through each stage of your hiring process - from initial screen to phone interview to onsite to offer. It's different from time-to-hire because it shows you _where_ candidates stall, not just the total elapsed time. Tracking recruitment funnel conversion rates at each stage - applicant-to-screen, screen-to-interview, and interview-to-hire - pinpoints the exact handoff where qualified talent drops off.

Appcast's [2026 Recruitment Marketing Benchmarks Report](https://info.appcast.io/whitepaper/2026-recruitment-marketing-benchmarks-report), built on 302 million job ad clicks from 2025, gives a median for each stage. Only 4.66% of clicks become applications, and 14.98% of applicants get screened. From there, 61.33% of screened candidates reach an interview, 40.45% of interviews produce an offer, and 75.21% of offers become hires.

**Formula:** Pipeline Velocity = (Candidates in Stage x Conversion Rate) / Days in Stage

**How to improve it:** Map your pipeline stage by stage and identify the bottleneck. Is it sourcing? Screening? Scheduling? Then automate that specific stage. The biggest velocity gains come from compressing the outreach-to-response and response-to-interview windows - the two stages where most candidates silently drop off.

## What Does a Recruitment Dashboard Template Look Like?

A working recruitment dashboard template groups the 12 recruiter KPIs into five panels, and each panel answers one question for one audience. Here's the layout we'd start from:

| Dashboard panel | Question it answers | KPIs on the panel | Best visual | Review cadence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Speed | Are we hiring fast enough? | Time-to-fill, time-to-hire | Weekly trend line | Weekly |
| Funnel | Where do candidates drop out? | Applicant-to-interview, interview-to-offer, pipeline velocity | Stage conversion bars | Weekly |
| Sources | Which channels produce hires? | Source of hire, cost-per-hire | Stacked bar by channel | Monthly |
| Outcomes | Are the hires working out? | Quality of hire, offer acceptance, 90-day retention, candidate NPS | Scorecards with quarter-over-quarter change | Monthly or quarterly |
| Capacity | Can the team absorb more reqs? | Recruiter-to-req ratio | One number per recruiter | Weekly |

Put Speed and Funnel at the top of the page. They move week to week and drive standup conversations. Outcomes belong lower down: they lag by months, so leadership checks them in quarterly reviews.

### Can You Build a Recruitment Dashboard in Excel?

Yes. For teams with fewer than a few dozen open reqs, a spreadsheet is often the right first version. Use three tabs:

1. **Raw data.** An export from your ATS with one row per candidate per stage, including dates, source, and status.
2. **Calculations.** Each KPI computed with the formulas above, referencing the raw tab.
3. **Dashboard.** Pivot charts that read only from the calculations tab, so nobody edits a chart by hand.

Spreadsheets break once someone has to re-paste exports every Monday morning. At that point, move to your ATS's built-in reporting or a BI tool connected to it. ## How Do You Build a Recruitment KPIs Dashboard?

Having 12 KPIs is useless if they live in separate spreadsheets. Here's how to put them together into a dashboard that actually drives decisions.

### Start With Four, Then Expand

Don't try to track all 12 from day one. Start with the four that give you the most signal: time-to-fill, cost-per-hire, offer acceptance rate, and quality of hire. These four alone will tell you whether your process is fast enough, affordable enough, and producing hires that stick.

Once those four are stable and you've benchmarked your baselines, add source of hire and 90-day retention. These tell you _why_ your top-line metrics look the way they do. Then add the remaining six as your data infrastructure matures.

### Choose the Right Reporting Cadence

Not every KPI needs the same review frequency. Pipeline metrics like time-to-fill, applicant-to-interview ratio, and pipeline velocity change week to week - review these in weekly standups so you can catch bottlenecks before they compound. A one-week delay in spotting a scheduling bottleneck turns into a three-week time-to-fill increase by the time you notice.

Quality and cost metrics need more data to be meaningful. Cost-per-hire, quality of hire, and 90-day retention should be reviewed monthly or quarterly. Monthly reviews give you enough data points to spot trends without overreacting to single-hire outliers. Set formal benchmarks each quarter and recalibrate your targets annually based on how your team, market, and tech stack have changed.

Candidate NPS is the exception - survey immediately after each hiring process, but aggregate and analyze monthly. Individual scores fluctuate too much to be actionable. Monthly trends tell you whether your candidate experience is improving or declining.

### Set Benchmarks, Not Targets

Use the industry benchmarks in this article as reference points, not goals. Your ideal time-to-fill depends on your roles, your market, and your hiring bar. A 60-day time-to-fill for a principal engineer might be excellent. A 60-day time-to-fill for an SDR is a problem. Set internal benchmarks based on your own historical data, then measure improvement quarter over quarter.

| KPI | Industry Benchmark | What "Good" Looks Like | Red Flag |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Time-to-Fill | 39 days (SHRM median) | Under 35 days | Over 60 days |
| Time-to-Hire | 1-2 weeks under time-to-fill | Under 28 days | Over 50 days |
| Cost-Per-Hire | $5,475 (SHRM average) | Under $4,000 | Over $7,000 |
| Offer Acceptance Rate | 80%+ | Over 90% | Below 75% |
| Quality of Hire | Composite score | Above 80/100 | Below 60/100 |
| 90-Day Retention | 85%+ | Over 90% | Below 80% |
| Candidate NPS | +17 | Above +30 | Below 0 |
| Interview-to-Offer | 3:1 | Under 3:1 | Over 5:1 |
| Source of Hire | Varies | 40%+ from outbound | 80%+ from job boards |
| Recruiter-to-Req | Up 67% at XL orgs (SHRM 2026) | 15:1 or lower | Over 30:1 |
| Applicant-to-Interview | 8-12:1 | Under 10:1 | Over 25:1 |
| Pipeline Velocity | Varies by stage | No stage over 7 days | Any stage over 14 days |

### Connect KPIs to Each Other

The real value of a dashboard is spotting connections between metrics. A rising time-to-fill with a stable interview-to-offer ratio? That's a sourcing bottleneck. A dropping offer acceptance rate with steady time-to-hire? Probably a compensation issue. A low 90-day retention rate despite high quality-of-hire scores? Your onboarding might be the problem, not your recruiting.

Every KPI is more useful in context than in isolation. Build your dashboard so that when one metric moves, you can immediately see which related metrics explain why.

### Avoid the Vanity Metric Trap

Some metrics look impressive on a slide deck but don't drive decisions. Total applications received, number of interviews conducted, and recruiter activity volume (emails sent, calls made) are vanity metrics unless tied to outcomes. A recruiter who sends 200 generic outreach messages isn't outperforming one who sends 50 targeted messages that earn 5x the replies.

The 12 KPIs in this guide were chosen specifically because each one connects to a hiring outcome: speed, quality, cost, or conversion. If a metric doesn't help you make a decision - staff up, fix a process, shift budget, change channels - it doesn't belong on your dashboard.

### What is People Analytics?

People analytics is the practice of using workforce data, from recruiting funnels to performance and retention, to make HR decisions with evidence instead of instinct. A recruitment dashboard is its most common starting point, because hiring data is already structured inside the ATS and moves weekly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are recruitment KPIs?

Recruitment KPIs are the measurable targets a hiring team uses to judge speed, quality, cost, and throughput. The 12 recruiter KPIs in this guide cover all four, from time-to-fill and source of hire to quality of hire and pipeline velocity. Each one should connect to a decision, such as shifting budget between channels or fixing a slow interview stage.

### What should be on a recruiting dashboard?

A recruiting dashboard needs five panels: speed (time-to-fill, time-to-hire), funnel (stage conversion rates), sources (source of hire, cost-per-hire), outcomes (quality of hire, offer acceptance, retention, candidate NPS), and capacity (recruiter-to-req ratio). Review speed and funnel weekly and outcomes monthly or quarterly. Adding a time-to-first-reply metric helps too: in Pin's data, 89% of candidate replies arrive within 72 hours.

### What are the most important recruiter KPIs to track first?

Start with four: time-to-fill, cost-per-hire, offer acceptance rate, and quality of hire. These give you the clearest picture of hiring speed, cost efficiency, and outcome quality. SHRM puts the median nonexecutive time-to-fill at 39 days (2026) and the average nonexecutive cost-per-hire at $5,475 (2025). Use those as initial reference points, then benchmark against your own historical data.

### How do you measure quality of hire?

Quality of hire is a composite metric combining performance ratings at 6-12 months, hiring manager satisfaction, and first-year retention. A common weighting is 40% performance, 30% manager satisfaction, and 30% retention. According to LinkedIn's 2025 Future of Recruiting report, 61% of TA professionals believe AI will improve how teams measure quality of hire by connecting sourcing data to long-term outcomes.

### What is a good interview-to-offer ratio?

A healthy interview-to-offer ratio is 3:1 - three interviews for every one offer extended. Anything above 4:1 suggests your screening process is letting too many borderline candidates through to the interview stage. Organizations that reach this benchmark invest in stronger pre-interview screening, not faster interviewing. AI-powered matching helps here: Pin's 83% candidate acceptance rate means fewer borderline candidates ever reach the interview stage in the first place.

### How does AI affect recruiter KPIs?

AI tools compress the non-judgment parts of recruiting: sourcing, screening, outreach, and scheduling. Teams using AI save roughly 20% of their work week according to LinkedIn's 2025 data. The biggest KPI shifts come in time-to-fill, cost-per-hire, and recruiter-to-req capacity. Pin users typically fill positions in about two weeks compared to SHRM's 39-day median.

### How often should you review recruiter KPIs?

Review pipeline KPIs (time-to-fill, pipeline velocity, applicant-to-interview ratio) weekly. Review quality and cost KPIs (quality of hire, cost-per-hire, 90-day retention) monthly or quarterly, since they need more data to be meaningful. Set formal benchmarks quarterly and recalibrate annually. The goal isn't perfect scores on every metric - it's consistent improvement quarter over quarter.

## Key Takeaways

-   Track KPIs across all four categories - pipeline speed, hire quality, cost efficiency, and throughput - not just the two or three most visible metrics
-   Use [industry benchmarks](/blog/sourcing-benchmarks-report/) (39-day median time-to-fill, $5,475 cost-per-hire, 80%+ offer acceptance) as reference points, not as targets. Your ideal numbers depend on your roles and market.
-   Connect KPIs to each other. A rising time-to-fill with stable interview ratios is a sourcing problem. A dropping acceptance rate with stable time-to-hire is a compensation problem.
-   AI tools don't just improve individual metrics - they break the traditional tradeoff between speed and quality by automating the time-consuming steps that don't require human judgment
-   Start with four KPIs, get your baselines solid, then expand to all 12 as your data infrastructure matures
