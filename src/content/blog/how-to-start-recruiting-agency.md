Knowing how to start a recruiting agency comes down to eight essentials. You'll need a niche, a legal entity, a business bank account, recruiter liability insurance, a CRM (customer relationship manager) or ATS (applicant tracking system), an AI sourcing tool, a pricing model, and your first client. You can launch from a home office for under $2,000 or build a full-scale operation for $65,000-$158,000, depending on how fast you want to grow. Either way, the staffing industry - worth roughly $190 billion in the US alone, according to [Staffing Industry Analysts (2025)](https://www.staffingindustry.com/news/global-daily-news/forecast-estimates-us-staffing-industrys-cumulative-growth-by-2030) - has room for new entrants who bring specialization and speed.

Around 25,000 staffing and recruiting companies already operate in the US, per the [American Staffing Association](https://americanstaffing.net/research/fact-sheets-analysis-staffing-industry-trends/staffing-industry-statistics/). That sounds like a crowded market. It isn't. Most agencies are generalists competing on price, and the firms using AI to source and engage candidates are pulling ahead fast. According to [Bullhorn's 2026 GRID Report](https://www.bullhorn.com/news-and-press/press-releases/bullhorn-grid-report-staffing-firms-using-ai-see-stronger-growth-faster-placements/), top-performing staffing firms are 4x more likely to use AI, and firms using AI are 90% more likely to place candidates within 20 days.

This guide walks through every step - from paperwork to your first placement - with real numbers, not vague advice. If you're weighing whether to go solo first, our guide on [how to work as a freelance recruiter](https://www.pin.com/blog/freelance-recruiter-guide/) covers the lighter-weight path.

> **TL;DR:**
>
> - **Eight-step launch plan.** Pick a niche, form a legal entity, set up insurance and banking, build your tech stack, choose a fee model, land a first client, scale with AI, and avoid the common failure points.
> - **Startup budget is flexible.** Expect $2K for a bootstrapped solo launch and up to $158K+ for a fully staffed office ([Upmetrics](https://upmetrics.co/startup-costs/staffing-agency)).
> - **Niche beats generalist every time.** Most of the 25,000 US agencies compete on price; specialists pulling from a specific vertical close faster and charge more.
> - **Fees are predictable.** Contingent 15-25% of first-year salary is standard; retained 25-35% paid in stages is the model for executive search.
> - **AI changes the competitive math.** Top staffing firms are 4x more likely to use AI ([Bullhorn 2026 GRID](https://www.bullhorn.com/news-and-press/press-releases/bullhorn-grid-report-staffing-firms-using-ai-see-stronger-growth-faster-placements/)), and AI-using firms were 90% more likely to place candidates within 20 days in Bullhorn's 2025 GRID. ## How Much Does It Cost to Start a Recruiting Agency?

Startup costs for a recruiting agency run from $400 at the bootstrapped end to over $158,000 for a fully staffed office, per founder data from [Starter Story (2025)](https://www.starterstory.com/ideas/recruiting-agency/profitability) and [Upmetrics](https://upmetrics.co/startup-costs/staffing-agency). One founder launched Devrize with just $400 and generated $302,100 in first-year revenue. That's an extreme case, but it proves the floor is low.

Here's a realistic breakdown for three different launch profiles:

| Launch Type | Startup Cost Range | Key Expenses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solo/Home-Based | $2,000–$6,000 | LLC formation ($50–$500), insurance ($500–$1,200/yr), AI sourcing tool ($0–$100/mo), CRM ($0–$99/mo), website ($0–$200), phone/email ($20–$50/mo) |
| Small Office | $15,000–$40,000 | Everything above, plus office lease ($500–$2,000/mo), professional website ($2,000–$5,000), job boards ($200–$500/mo), 6-month marketing budget ($3,000–$6,000) |
| Full-Scale Operation | $65,000–$158,000+ | Office build-out, 2–5 recruiter salaries (largest expense), enterprise <a href="https://www.pin.com/blog/recruitment-agency-software/">recruiting software</a>, legal and accounting setup |

For most first-time owners, starting solo or with one partner is the right call. No fancy office is required to make placements. All you need is a phone, a sourcing tool, and clients. Solo recruiters who handle both client development and candidate delivery are essentially running a [360 recruitment model](https://www.pin.com/blog/what-is-360-recruitment/) - managing the full desk from business development through placement.

## Step 1: Pick a Niche (This Decides Everything Else)

Niche agencies outperform generalists on nearly every metric. Agencies averaging $1.22M in annual revenue, per [Starter Story (2025)](https://www.starterstory.com/ideas/recruiting-agency/profitability), are overwhelmingly specialists, not firms that recruit for "any role in any industry." Your niche determines your fee structure, your clients, your sourcing strategy, and your competitive moat.

How do you pick? Start with these three filters:

**1\. Where do you have relationships?** If you spent five years in healthcare IT, you already know the hiring managers, the job titles, the salary bands, and the candidate pools. Research alone can't replace that kind of head start.

**2\. Where's the demand?** High-turnover industries (healthcare, logistics, hospitality) need constant pipeline. Hard-to-fill specialties (cybersecurity, AI/ML engineering, cleared government roles) command premium fees. Both work - pick the one that matches your background.

**3\. Where can you charge more?** Standard placement fees range from 15-25% of first-year salary, with executive and specialized search commanding 25-35%, per [a 2025 staffing fee analysis](https://talentleverage.com/resources/how-much-does-it-cost-to-hire-a-recruiter-2025/). At 20%, a $150K software engineer placement nets $30,000. With a $45K warehouse worker at 15%, the fee comes to $6,750. Both are valid businesses, but the unit economics look very different.

Some niches that consistently produce strong margins for new agencies: DevOps and cloud infrastructure roles, healthcare IT, cybersecurity, AI/ML engineering, cleared government contractors, and fintech compliance. Each of these has a shortage of qualified candidates, which means employers will pay premium fees and won't haggle on your rate.

Don't try to be everything. Anyone who "specializes in tech, healthcare, finance, and manufacturing" specializes in nothing.

We've noticed that the agencies hitting $300K+ in year one almost always make the same call first: they pick a niche narrow enough to develop genuine expertise in.

Across Pin's agency customer base, the founders who close placements fastest chose a specialty they understand at a technical level. They can qualify candidates without a job description in front of them. That depth - knowing what "strong" looks like for a DevOps engineer or a pharma compliance specialist - matters more than any sourcing tool, marketing budget, or [structured recruiter training](/blog/recruiter-training/) on its own.

Equally telling: new agencies that start narrow almost always expand later. Agencies that start broad rarely survive long enough to narrow. If you have domain expertise, use it. If you don't, pick the highest-demand vertical adjacent to your strongest existing relationships. Expertise plus network access is what moves you from your first client call to your first placement in the shortest possible time. Instead of treating "niche" as a limitation, they treat it as their most powerful filter for qualifying clients, pricing with confidence, and building a referral network. That mindset - niche first, scale later - is central to how to start a recruiting agency that survives past year two.

## Step 2: Form Your Legal Entity

About 20.4% of US businesses fail in their first year, according to [Bureau of Labor Statistics data (2024)](https://www.bls.gov/bdm/bdmage.htm). Proper legal structure won't save a bad business model, but it protects your personal assets if things go sideways. Here's what you need:

**Business structure:** Most recruiting agencies start as an LLC (limited liability company), which is simple, protects personal assets, and offers tax flexibility. If you plan to bring on partners or raise capital later, you can convert to an S-Corp or C-Corp. Talk to an accountant before choosing - the tax implications differ by state and income level.

**EIN (Employer Identification Number):** Free from the IRS. Takes five minutes online. You'll need it for your business bank account, tax filings, and client contracts.

**Business bank account:** Keep personal and business finances completely separate from day one. This isn't optional - it's how you maintain your LLC's liability protection.

**Insurance:** At minimum, you require general liability insurance and professional liability (errors & omissions) coverage. If you're placing temporary workers, you'll also require workers' compensation insurance. Budget $500-$1,200/year for basic coverage.

**Contracts:** You need two standard agreements before making your first call: a client service agreement (your terms with the employer) and a candidate placement agreement (terms around guarantee periods, replacement clauses, and fee schedules). Hire a lawyer for the first draft. Template contracts from the internet will cost you more in the long run when a client disputes a fee.

## Step 3: Set Up Your Business Plan

Business plans for recruiting agencies don't need to be long. Five questions cover everything: niche, year-one revenue target, monthly burn rate, number of active clients, and a 90-day plan. That's it.

Recruiting agencies that survive past year five - 51.6% of all businesses, per [BLS data (2024)](https://www.bls.gov/bdm/bdmage.htm) - typically started with clear revenue targets and a realistic timeline to hit them. Once you're past the startup phase, the playbook shifts from survival to scaling - our guide to [recruitment agency growth strategies](https://www.pin.com/blog/recruitment-agency-growth-strategies/) covers what drives revenue acceleration from year two onward.

## Step 5: Choose Your Fee Model

Most new recruiting agencies should start with contingency search at 15–25% of first-year salary. It's the lowest-risk model for building an initial client base, because clients only pay when you deliver a hire.

The average cost per hire is $5,475 for non-executive roles, according to SHRM's 2025 Recruiting Benchmarking Report. That's what companies spend internally. Your job is to deliver better candidates faster than their internal team can - and charge accordingly. Our [guide to agency commission structures](https://www.pin.com/blog/recruitment-agency-commission-structures/) covers every model in detail, including hybrid arrangements and retainer structures.

Three fee models dominate the agency world:

### Contingency (Most Common for New Agencies)

You only get paid when your candidate gets hired. Standard rate: 15-25% of first-year salary. Pros: easy to sell to new clients, no upfront commitment from them. Cons: you carry all the risk, and clients can ghost you after you've submitted candidates.

### Retained Search

The client pays an upfront fee (typically 1/3 of the total), another third at candidate shortlist, and the final third at hire. Standard rate: 25-35% of first-year salary. Pros: guaranteed revenue, serious clients only. Cons: harder to sell, especially as a new agency without a track record.

### Contract/Temp Staffing

You employ the worker and bill the client a markup over the worker's pay rate. Typical markups run 25-40%, per [The Resource Company (2025)](https://www.theresource.com/2025/10/27/average-staffing-agency-markup-in-2025/). Pros: recurring revenue, predictable cash flow. Cons: you're on the hook for payroll, benefits, and workers' comp. Requires more capital to start.

New agencies almost universally start with contingency search, which requires no upfront commitment from the client and pays only when a hire is made. It's the lowest barrier to entry and the easiest pitch to skeptical hiring managers. As you build a reputation, introduce retained components - even partial retainers (a small upfront fee + contingency remainder) signal that you're serious and filter out tire-kickers.

| Fee Model | Typical Rate | When You Get Paid | Risk Level | Best For |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Contingency | 15-25% of salary | After candidate starts | High (you carry all risk) | New agencies building client base |
| Retained Search | 25-35% of salary | 1/3 upfront, 1/3 shortlist, 1/3 hire | Low (guaranteed partial payment) | Established agencies, executive roles |
| Contract/Temp Staffing | 25-40% markup on pay rate | Ongoing (weekly/biweekly billing) | Medium (payroll obligation) | Agencies with capital for payroll |

One pricing mistake new agency owners make: not having a written fee schedule ready before the first client meeting. Know your rates, your guarantee period, and your payment terms cold. Negotiating on the fly signals inexperience and costs you money every time.

## Step 8: Avoid the Mistakes That Kill New Agencies

Only 51.6% of businesses survive to year five, per [BLS data (2024)](https://www.bls.gov/bdm/bdmage.htm). In recruiting, the failure modes are predictable - and avoidable:

**Mistake 1: Going too broad.** "We recruit for everyone" means you recruit for no one well. Specialization isn't just a marketing strategy - it determines your sourcing efficiency, your candidate quality, and your ability to charge premium fees.

**Mistake 2: Underpricing to win clients.** Dropping your fee from 20% to 12% to land a deal sounds smart until you realize you need nearly twice as many placements to hit the same revenue. Compete on speed and quality, not price. Hiring companies that choose you on price will leave you on price.

**Mistake 3: No written contracts.** A handshake deal works until it doesn't. Before you start working any requisition, get signed agreements that specify the fee percentage, guarantee period (typically 60-90 days), payment terms (net 30 is standard), and what happens if the candidate quits or is terminated.

**Mistake 4: Ignoring cash flow.** Contingency recruiting has a cash flow problem: you do the work in month one, but the client, who typically owes payment 30 days after the candidate's start date, pays in month two or three (if you're lucky). Build a 3-month cash reserve before going full-time. If you're placing temporary workers, the cash gap is even worse - you're paying the worker weekly while waiting 30-60 days for client payment. Look into staffing-specific financing (payroll factoring) if you go the temp route.

**Mistake 5: Manual everything.** Rich Rosen, an executive recruiter at Cornerstone Search with 29+ years in the industry, put it simply: "In 6 months I can directly attribute over $250K in revenue to Pin." New agencies that try to source candidates manually are competing against AI-powered firms with one hand tied behind their back. The math doesn't work.

**Mistake 6: No financial buffer.** Even if you land a client in week one, your first payment might not arrive for 60-90 days. Contingency fees are typically paid 30 days after the candidate's start date, and some clients stretch it further. Have at least three months of personal living expenses saved before you quit your day job. Six months is better. The agencies that fail in year one rarely fail because they couldn't find clients - they fail because they ran out of money waiting for invoices to clear.
